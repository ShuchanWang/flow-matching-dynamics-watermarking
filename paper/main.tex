\title{Dynamics-Level Watermarking of Flow Matching Models with Random Codes}
\author{Shuchan Wang}
\date{\today}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We embed keyed, multi-bit watermarks in the velocity fields of flow
matching models and recover them through black-box velocity queries. The
method treats watermarking as random coding over a continuous channel and
uses a temporal carrier for synchronous message recovery. An information
bound relates code geometry, noise, and query count, and a first-order
perturbation analysis bounds terminal-distribution drift. Across image and
audio generators, the watermark recovers the message with quality ratios
close to one, and it persists under compression, moderate adaptation, and
velocity-supervised distillation. It is removed by carrier subtraction or by enough full-parameter updates, a limit shared by other watermarking approaches on open checkpoints. It therefore serves non-adversarial auditing, including license verification, leak detection, and attribution of
deployed or distilled copies, rather than enforcement against a recipient
who removes the mark.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Flow Matching and Model Ownership}

Flow matching~\cite{lipmanflow,liuflow} trains an ODE-based generator
whose velocity field $v_\theta(x,t)$ transports a simple noise
distribution $p_{\mathrm{noise}}$ toward a data distribution
$q_{\mathrm{data}}$:
\begin{equation}
\frac{dx_t}{dt}=v_\theta(x_t,t),\qquad t\in[0,1].
\end{equation}
For straight interpolation $x_t=(1-t)x_0+tx_1$, training regresses the
predicted velocity against $x_1-x_0$. Sampling integrates the learned
field from an initial noise state. The velocity field therefore
provides a model-level object in which to encode an ownership message.

A useful ownership mark carries multiple bits, preserves generation
quality, and can be recovered through a specified verification
interface. We consider black-box access to velocity responses, without
inspecting model weights.

\subsection{Relation to Existing Watermarks}\label{sec:related}

Existing methods encode ownership in different objects.

Tree-Ring modifies sampling noise and detects the mark by inverting a
generated image through the diffusion model~\cite{wen2023tree}.
Stable Signature fine-tunes the latent decoder and reads the signature
with an image watermark extractor~\cite{fernandez2023stable}.
Both verify generated content, but use different extraction procedures.

Weight-level methods embed bits in selected parameters and require
white-box extraction~\cite{uchida2017embedding}. Verification inspects
weights, so the mark is tied to a particular parameterization.

Trigger-based methods encode ownership in responses to designated
inputs and support black-box verification~\cite{adi2018turning,chen2019blackmarks,fei2025high}.
For diffusion generators, SleeperMark trains triggered watermark
responses to persist through downstream fine-tuning by disentangling
watermark information from semantic concepts~\cite{wang2024sleepermark}.

These methods target generated content, weights, or designated
responses. We instead encode and recover a message in the learned
velocity field. Appendix~\ref{app:comparison} compares access,
cost, and persistence under modification.

\subsection{The Gap: Dynamics-Level Watermarking}

The continuous velocity field $v_\theta(x,t)$ offers a
high-dimensional channel for embedding information through structured
perturbations while preserving generation quality.

A velocity-field formulation separates the encoding from the image
decoder, sampling-noise construction, and network parameterization.
The same construction applies to continuous velocity fields across
architectures and modalities; we evaluate image and audio generators.

This gap motivates our core question:
\begin{quote}
\emph{Can we embed a verifiable, keyed, multi-bit message directly into
the learned velocity field of a flow matching model, such that (1) the
watermark is detectable with only black-box access, (2) generation
quality is preserved, and (3) reliable detection requires access to a
secret key?}
\end{quote}

We address this question in three parts. Section~\ref{sec:wmrc}
constructs keyed, multi-bit signals in the velocity field, defines the
detection procedure, and proves the design properties. Section~\ref{sec:security_analysis}
analyzes what a party without the key can infer. Section~\ref{sec:experiments}
evaluates detection, quality preservation, false-positive control,
payload, verification cost, and robustness across image and audio
generators.

\section{Watermarking as Random Coding over Transport Dynamics}
\label{sec:wmrc}
\subsection{Information-Theoretic Motivation}

From an information-theoretic perspective, our approach views the velocity
field as a continuous additive channel. Let $v_\theta(x,t)$ be the clean
velocity field for a $D$-dimensional state $x\in\mathbb R^D$ and time
$t\in[0,1]$, and consider a watermarked version:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vwm}
v_{\theta,K,m}(x,t) = v_\theta(x,t) + \eta(x,t) + \epsilon \cdot s_{K,m}(x,t),
\end{equation}
where $\eta(x,t)$ represents background variation from training
stochasticity and $s_{K,m}(x,t)$ is a key-dependent signal encoding
message $m$, with watermark strength $\epsilon>0$.

The detection problem becomes: given $N$ noisy observations of
$v_{\theta,K,m}(x_i,t_i)$, estimate $\hat{m}$ with high confidence.
The coding viewpoint~\cite{cover1999elements} separates message
geometry from background velocity variation. The analysis below states
the assumptions on that variation explicitly.

\subsection{Message and Key: Framework}

We specify messages through keyed signals $s_{K,m}(x,t)$ in
\eqref{eq:vwm}.

Let $m \in \mathcal{M}$ be a message from a set of
$|\mathcal{M}| := 2^L$ possible messages, where $L$ is the payload length
in bits, and let $K$ be a secret key.
The key defines a codebook through a mapping
\begin{equation}
\phi_K : \mathcal{M} \to \mathcal{H},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{H}$ is a function space over $(x,t)$. The codeword for
message $m$ is $s_{K,m}(x,t) := \phi_K(m)$. We use the
$L^2$ norm over the query distribution $p_q(x,t)$ for $\mathcal H$:
\[
\|s\|_{\mathcal H}^2:=\mathbb E_{(x,t)\sim p_q}\|s(x,t)\|_2^2.
\]


To quantify distributional distortion, let $\rho(x,t)$ be the clean-flow
density and $\|f\|_{\TV}:=\int_{\mathbb R^D}|f(x)|\,dx$ the
total-variation norm of a signed density. Write $\rho^w_{K,m}$ for the
density induced by $v_\theta+\epsilon s_{K,m}$ from the same initial
distribution as the clean flow.

\begin{definition}[Admissible codebook]\label{def:admissible}
A codebook $\{s_{K,m}\}_{m\in\mathcal{M}}$ is \textbf{admissible}
at strength $\epsilon$ and prescribed terminal-distortion budget
$\delta_{\mathrm{dist}}>0$, relative to the clean flow, if it satisfies
the following conditions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Bounded distributional drift.} For every $m\in\mathcal M$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:admissible-drift}
\bigl\|\rho^w_{K,m}(\cdot,1)-\rho(\cdot,1)\bigr\|_{\TV}
\le\delta_{\mathrm{dist}}.
\end{equation}
\item \textbf{Message separation.} Distinct messages have distinct
codewords, with minimum distance
\begin{equation}
\Delta_K:=\min_{m\ne m'}\|s_{K,m}-s_{K,m'}\|_{\mathcal H}>0.
\end{equation}
\item \textbf{Energy normalization.} There exists a constant $E_s > 0$
such that for all $m \in \mathcal{M}$,
\begin{equation}
\|s_{K,m}\|_{\mathcal H}^2 = E_s.
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

The first condition specifies the desired distributional preservation
directly. Theorem~\ref{thm:drift-main} gives a sufficient small-strength
regime using the drift bound proved in Appendix~\ref{app:preservation},
Theorem~\ref{thm:drift}. The experiments assess quality preservation in
image and audio feature space. Message separation requires different
signals for different messages, while energy normalization gives every
message the same signal energy.

\subsection{Concrete Construction}\label{sec:concrete}

We construct hypercube and orthogonal codebooks. Both map a message to
a unit codeword, then apply a secret projection and a temporal carrier
to obtain the watermark signal.
The secret key is $K = (P, C)$, where $C$ specifies this mapping and
$P \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times k}$ is a semi-orthogonal projection matrix
satisfying $P^T P = I_k$, generated by QR decomposition of a random matrix
seeded by the key. It maps a codeword $c_m\in\mathbb R^k$ to the
$D$-dimensional velocity direction $Pc_m$ without changing its norm.

The hypercube encoding assigns each bit to a signed coordinate.
For an $L$-bit message $m=(m_1,\ldots,m_L)$ with $L\leq k$, let
$e_1,\ldots,e_L$ be the first $L$ standard basis vectors in
$\mathbb R^k$ and set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hypercube-codeword}
c_m=\frac{1}{\sqrt L}\sum_{j=1}^L(2m_j-1)e_j.
\end{equation}
Each bit selects the sign of one coordinate; the factor $1/\sqrt L$
gives unit energy. The $2^L$ codewords are represented implicitly by
these axes, and decoding reads the signs of the demodulated coordinates.
The ambient directions $Pe_j$ are secret-key dependent. This encoding
supports $L$ bits whenever $L\leq k$, without storing or searching
$2^L$ codewords.

The orthogonal encoding instead assigns a separate direction to each
message. When $2^L\leq k$, QR decomposition of $2^L$ Gaussian-sampled vectors
in $\mathbb R^k$ produces unit codewords with
$\langle c_m,c_{m'}\rangle=0$ for $m\ne m'$. Each message is assigned
one of these codewords and decoded by maximum correlation.
This encoding supports at most $\lfloor\log_2 k\rfloor$ bits.

Throughout the paper, let
\begin{equation}
\alpha(t) = \sin(2\pi t)
\end{equation}
denote the scalar temporal carrier. We define the watermark signal for
message $m$ as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:watermark_signal}
s_{K,m}(x,t) = \alpha(t) \cdot P c_m.
\end{equation}
The keyed direction $P c_m$ carries the message, while $\alpha(t)$
enables synchronous demodulation.

\begin{proposition}[Signal energy and separation]\label{prop:energy-sep}
For either encoding above, the signal in \eqref{eq:watermark_signal}
has energy $E_s = 1/2$ and minimum separation
\[
\Delta_K = \begin{cases}
1, & \text{orthogonal code},\\
\sqrt{2/L}, & \text{hypercube code}.
\end{cases}
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since $P^TP = I_k$ and $\mathbb{E}[\alpha^2] = 1/2$,
$\|s_{K,m}\|^2 = \tfrac12\|Pc_m\|^2 = \tfrac12$, giving $E_s = 1/2$.
For separation,
$\|s_{K,m}-s_{K,m'}\|^2 = \tfrac12\|c_m-c_{m'}\|^2$.
Orthogonal codewords give $\|c_m-c_{m'}\|^2 = 2$, hence signal
distance $1$. For hypercube messages with Hamming distance $h$,
$\|c_m-c_{m'}\|^2 = 4h/L$, hence signal distance $\sqrt{2h/L}$, whose
minimum over $h\ge1$ is $\sqrt{2/L}$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Distributional Preservation}\label{sec:preservation}

The remaining design property is bounded distributional drift, which
requires the transport analysis below. Let
$\mathcal{T}_{1\leftarrow r}f$ denote the terminal signed density
obtained by transporting an integrable signed density $f$ from time
$r$ to time $1$ under the clean field $v_\theta$, i.e., solving
$\partial_t g_t+\nabla_x\!\cdot(g_t v_\theta)=0$ on $[r,1]$ with
$g_r=f$. Its maximal amplification is assumed finite and defined by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:transport-amplification}
M_T:=\sup_{0\le r\le1}\sup_{f\ne0}
\frac{\|\mathcal{T}_{1\leftarrow r}f\|_{\TV}}{\|f\|_{\TV}}<\infty.
\end{equation}
The constant $M_T$ is defined by the clean field $v_\theta$ and does
not depend on the watermark strength $\epsilon$, the key, or the
message.

\begin{theorem}[Bounded distributional drift]\label{thm:drift-main}
Assume uniform query time, either encoding of
Section~\ref{sec:concrete}, and the regularity of
Theorem~\ref{thm:drift} in Appendix~\ref{app:preservation}. For each
message, the induced terminal density obeys
\begin{equation}
\bigl\|\rho^w_{K,m}(\cdot,1)-\rho(\cdot,1)\bigr\|_{\TV}
\le\epsilon M_T\int_0^1
\bigl\|\nabla\!\cdot(\rho(\cdot,r)s_{K,m}(\cdot,r))\bigr\|_{\TV}\,dr
+O(\epsilon^2),
\end{equation}
where the $O(\epsilon^2)$ term has TV norm bounded by a constant times
$\epsilon^2$ as $\epsilon\to0$. If the forcing integrals are finite for
all messages, then for any fixed $\delta_{\mathrm{dist}}>0$ the
codebook is admissible for sufficiently small $\epsilon>0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
In Appendix~\ref{app:preservation}, the linearized continuity equation
\eqref{eq:linearized-continuity} identifies
$-\nabla\!\cdot(\rho s_{K,m})$ as the first-order density forcing.
Transporting this forcing to time $1$ and applying the TV norm gives
\eqref{eq:general-drift-bound} in Theorem~\ref{thm:drift}.
The linear term in this bound and the higher-order $O(\epsilon^2)$ term
both tend to zero as $\epsilon\to0$. Because the message set is finite
and every forcing integral is finite, a common sufficiently small
$\epsilon$ makes the full bound at most $\delta_{\mathrm{dist}}$ for
every message, establishing \eqref{eq:admissible-drift}.
\end{proof}

The carrier $\alpha(t)=\sin(2\pi t)$ has zero mean, which cancels the
time-invariant component of the transported density forcing
(Corollary~\ref{cor:zero-mean-gap}). The time-varying component is
bounded by the drift analysis in Appendix~\ref{app:preservation}.

\subsection{Watermarked Training}

We now describe how the watermark is embedded during flow matching
training. Given a training pair $(x_0, x_1)$ and interpolated state
$x_t = (1-t)x_0 + t x_1$, the standard flow matching target is
$U=x_1-x_0$. The clean population velocity is
$u(x,t)=\mathbb E[U\mid x_t=x,t]$. We modify the sampled target as
\begin{equation}
\tilde{v} = U + \epsilon_0 \cdot s_{K,m}(x_t, t)
= U + \epsilon_0 \sin(2\pi t) \cdot P c_m,
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon_0 > 0$ is the nominal perturbation amplitude applied
during training. The effective watermark strength $\epsilon$ in \eqref{eq:vwm} may differ from $\epsilon_0$,
because the $\mathcal{L}_{\text{wm}}$ term in \eqref{eq:loss} provides an additional
gradient that amplifies the effective
$\epsilon$.

The watermarked model $v_\theta$ is optimized with a combined objective:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:loss}
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{x_0,x_1,t}\left[\|v_\theta(x_t,t)
- \tilde{v}\|^2\right]}_{\mathcal{L}_{\text{vel}}}
+ \lambda \cdot \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{x_0,x_1,t}\left[-\sin(2\pi t) \cdot
\langle P^T v_\theta(x_t,t), c_m \rangle\right]}_{\mathcal{L}_{\text{wm}}},
\end{equation}
where $\lambda > 0$ balances the two terms. The first term
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{vel}}$ ensures the model learns the watermarked
velocity field. The second term $\mathcal{L}_{\text{wm}}$ directly rewards the model for producing velocity vectors whose demodulated projection aligns with the
target codeword $c_m$ (see Section~\ref{sec:detection}). The following proposition shows how these two terms combine to
determine the effective watermark strength.

\begin{proposition}[Population minimizer of the watermark objective]
\label{prop:optimal_predictor}
Assume expectations are finite and optimization is performed over all
square-integrable vector fields
$v:\mathbb{R}^D\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^D$.
Then the unique population minimizer of~\eqref{eq:loss} is
\begin{equation}
v^\star(x,t)
=
u(x,t)
+
\left(\epsilon_0+\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)
\sin(2\pi t)\,Pc_m.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Condition on $x_t=x$ and $t$. For a deterministic predicted velocity
$v$, the conditional expected objective differs by a constant from
\begin{equation}
\ell(v)=\|v-u(x,t)-\epsilon_0\alpha(t)Pc_m\|_2^2
-\lambda\alpha(t)\langle v,Pc_m\rangle.
\end{equation}
Its gradient is
$2(v-u-\epsilon_0\alpha(t)Pc_m)-\lambda\alpha(t)Pc_m$.
Setting this gradient to zero gives the stated minimizer. The
quadratic is strictly convex, so the minimizer is unique almost
everywhere under the training distribution.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The effective strength $\epsilon_0+\lambda/2$ in
Proposition~\ref{prop:optimal_predictor} describes the unconstrained
population minimizer. Finite data, model parameterization, and stochastic
optimization can change the strength learned in practice.
\end{remark}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Model Watermarking via Time-Modulated Random Coding}
\label{alg:watermark}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Input:} Training pairs $(x_0, x_1)$, key $K = (P, C)$, message $m$, 
\State \hspace{\algorithmicindent} watermark strength $\epsilon_0$, loss weight $\lambda$
\State Sample interpolation $x_t=(1-t)x_0+tx_1$ and conditional target $U=x_1-x_0$
\State Retrieve codeword $c_m$ from codebook $C$
\State Define time-modulated watermark: $s(x,t) \leftarrow \sin(2\pi t) \cdot P c_m$
\State Define watermarked training target: $\tilde{v}(x_t,t) \leftarrow U + \epsilon_0 \, s(x_t,t)$
\State Initialize model $v_\theta$
\For{each training step}
    \State Sample batch $\{(x_t^{(i)}, t^{(i)}, \tilde{v}^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^B$
    \State Forward pass: $\hat{v}^{(i)} \leftarrow v_\theta(x_t^{(i)}, t^{(i)})$
    \State Velocity loss: $\mathcal{L}_{\text{vel}} \leftarrow \frac{1}{B}\sum_i \|\hat{v}^{(i)} - \tilde{v}^{(i)}\|^2$
    \State Project: $z^{(i)} \leftarrow P^T \hat{v}^{(i)}$
    \State Demodulate: $d^{(i)} \leftarrow \sin(2\pi t^{(i)}) \cdot z^{(i)}$
    \State Watermark loss: $\mathcal{L}_{\text{wm}} \leftarrow -\frac{1}{B}\sum_i \langle d^{(i)}, c_m \rangle$
    \State Total loss: $\mathcal{L} \leftarrow \mathcal{L}_{\text{vel}} + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{\text{wm}}$
    \State Update $\theta$ via gradient descent
\EndFor
\State \textbf{Output:} Watermarked model $v_{\theta,K,m}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Detection via Synchronous Demodulation}\label{sec:detection}

At detection time, we are given a model $v$ (which may be watermarked
or clean) and the secret key $K = (P, C)$. Detection requires only
black-box query access to $v$.

We estimate the watermark signature via synchronous demodulation. Given
$N$ random query points $(x_i, t_i)$, the signature is computed as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:detection}
\hat{s} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \sin(2\pi t_i) \cdot P^T v(x_i, t_i).
\end{equation}
This estimator combines projection into the latent code space with
synchronous demodulation of the temporal carrier. The multiplication by
$\sin(2\pi t_i)$ extracts the time-coherent component while averaging out
background variation when its demodulated projection has zero mean
(Assumption~\ref{ass:uncorrelated}). The carrier's zero time average
alone does not ensure this property for a time-dependent velocity field.

The embedded message is recovered by maximum-correlation decoding:
\begin{equation}
\hat{m} = \arg\max_{m \in \mathcal{M}} \langle \hat{s}, c_m \rangle.
\end{equation}
For an explicit codebook, decoding compares all correlations. For the
hypercube, the same maximization reduces to reading the sign along each
bit axis, so no enumeration is needed. Codeword separation can shrink
as payload grows even when the total signal energy is unchanged.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Watermark Detection via Synchronous Demodulation}
\label{alg:detection}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Input:} Model $v$, key $K=(P,C)$, query count $N$, distribution $p_q(x,t)$
\State Sample $(x_i,t_i)\sim p_q$ independently, with uniform marginal query time
\State Query model: $y_i \leftarrow v(x_i, t_i)$ for $i = 1, \dots, N$
\State Project to code space: $z_i \leftarrow P^T y_i \in \mathbb{R}^k$
\State Demodulate with carrier: $d_i \leftarrow \sin(2\pi t_i) \cdot z_i$
\State Compute signature: $\hat{s} \leftarrow \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N d_i \in \mathbb{R}^k$
\State Decode message: $\hat{m} \leftarrow \arg\max_{m \in \mathcal{M}} \langle \hat{s}, c_m \rangle$
\State \textbf{Output:} Decoded message $\hat{m}$ and correlation score $\max_m \langle \hat{s}, c_m \rangle$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Under the additive-signal model, uniform query time, and the zero-mean
background and finite-variance assumptions in
Appendix~\ref{app:capacity_proof}, the signature has expectation
$\mathbb{E}[\hat{s}]=\frac{\epsilon}{2}c_m$ for a watermarked model
and zero for a clean model, with estimation error $O(1/\sqrt N)$.
The appendix derives these conditional statements; trained models can
have nonzero clean-background projections.

\subsection{Information Carried by the Demodulated Channel}

The projected velocity signature defines a noisy channel whose input is
one of the chosen message codewords. The nominal signaling space is the
$k$-dimensional subspace $\mathrm{span}(P)$. Because the velocity field
is produced by a nonlinear network, the response to training is not
confined to $\mathrm{span}(P)$, and the dimension the network
effectively uses can be smaller than $k$. The capacity below is for the
nominal channel.

The demodulated signature can be approximated by a $k$-dimensional
Gaussian channel: the background average satisfies a central limit
theorem, and the carrier average converges to $1/2$ by the law of large
numbers. Appendix~\ref{app:capacity_proof} derives this approximation.

\begin{theorem}[Capacity of the demodulated channel]
\label{thm:capacity}
Let $M$ be drawn from a finite message set and let $c_M\in\mathbb R^k$
be its unit-norm codeword. The limiting Gaussian channel for the
demodulated signature is
\begin{equation}
\hat{s} = \frac{\epsilon}{2}c_M+\xi,\qquad
\xi\sim\mathcal N(0,\Sigma/N),
\end{equation}
with $\xi$ independent of $M$ and $\Sigma$ a positive-definite noise
covariance before averaging over $N$ queries. Maximizing over all codeword covariances $Q:=\operatorname{Cov}(c_M)$ under the average unit-power constraint
$\operatorname{tr}Q\le 1$, the corresponding Gaussian channel has capacity
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C}
=
\max_{\substack{Q\succeq 0\\ \operatorname{tr}Q\le 1}}
\frac12\log\det\!\left(I_k+
\frac{N\epsilon^2}{4}\Sigma^{-1/2}Q\Sigma^{-1/2}\right).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
See Appendix~\ref{app:capacity_proof}.
\end{proof}

The maximum over all input covariances $Q$ can be attained by water-filling over the
eigenvalues of $\Sigma$~\cite{cover1999elements}. For uniform hypercube messages and isotropic noise
$\Sigma=\sigma^2 I_k$, sign decoding gives exact-message recovery
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hypercube-exact-recovery}
P_{\mathrm{exact}}(L)
=\left[\Phi\!\left(\frac{\epsilon\sqrt N}{2\sigma\sqrt L}\right)\right]^L,
\end{equation}
where $\Phi$ is the standard-normal CDF
(Appendix~\ref{app:hypercube-scaling}). At fixed $N$, $\epsilon$, and
$\sigma$, this probability decreases with $L$: each bit receives less
energy, and all $L$ decisions must succeed.

The two codebooks of Section~\ref{sec:concrete} distribute energy
differently. For uniform messages, the orthogonal codebook at $2^L=k$
has second moment $\mathbb E[c_Mc_M^T]=I_k/k$ and covariance
$Q=I_k/k-\bar c\bar c^T$, where
$\bar c=k^{-1}\sum_{m\in\mathcal M}c_m$.
The hypercube has zero mean and covariance
$Q=L^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^Le_je_j^T$.
For isotropic noise, $I_k/k$ is the maximizing covariance in the
Gaussian capacity expression; finite-codebook decoding additionally
depends on codeword geometry. Section~\ref{sec:experiments} varies $L$
at fixed $k$, $N$, and nominal signal strength to test the qualitative
payload effect in \eqref{eq:hypercube-exact-recovery}.

\section{Security Analysis}\label{sec:security_analysis}

Given the secret key $K$, the verifier extracts an unknown message from
velocity queries by synchronous demodulation (Section~\ref{sec:detection}).
Security concerns the
information available to a party without the key and the persistence
of the encoded message when the model is modified or copied. We first
specify this setting, then analyze key identifiability and the effects
of model modification and transfer.

\subsection{Threat Model}

Following model-ownership watermarking
formulations~\cite{uchida2017embedding,adi2018turning}, consider an owner
who embeds a message and releases the marked model while retaining
the key. A recipient may inspect or modify the released parameters,
query the model, or train a replacement from its responses. The
embedding and decoding algorithms are public. The recipient's goals
are to infer the key or encoded message, or to suppress message
recovery while preserving useful generation. The available
observations differ between access to generated samples and access
to intermediate velocities; this distinction governs transfer to a
replacement model.

\subsection{Key Identifiability}\label{sec:key-identifiability}

For a fixed embedded message, the carrier multiplies a single ambient
direction $q=Pc_m$. Even exact knowledge of $q$ leaves a family of
compatible projections and codebooks. The first result characterizes
the freedom in choosing the projected subspace; the second
characterizes equivalent coordinate systems within that subspace.

\begin{proposition}[Single-message subspace ambiguity]
\label{prop:subspace_search}
For $q\ne0$ and $1<k<D$, the family of $k$-dimensional subspaces
compatible with the direction $q$ is parameterized by the Grassmannian
$\operatorname{Gr}(k-1,D-1)$, of dimension
$(k-1)(D-k)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $U=\operatorname{span}(q)$. The quotient space
$\mathbb{R}^D/U$ has dimension $D-1$. For every $k$-dimensional
subspace $S$ containing $U$, the quotient $S/U$ is a $(k-1)$-dimensional
subspace of $\mathbb{R}^D/U$, and conversely the inverse image of
every such subspace is a $k$-dimensional subspace containing $U$.
The correspondence is bijective. The dimension of
$\operatorname{Gr}(k-1,D-1)$~\cite{zheng2002communication} is
$(k-1)((D-1)-(k-1))=(k-1)(D-k)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Orthogonal key ambiguity]\label{prop:P_hidden}
For every orthogonal $Q\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}$, the key pairs
$(P,C)$ and $(P Q,\{Q^T c_j\}_{j\in\mathcal M})$ produce the same
watermark signal for every message $j$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For each $j$, $(P Q)(Q^T c_j)=P(QQ^T)c_j=P c_j$.
The time-dependent carrier is therefore identical under both keys.
\end{proof}

Together, these results describe the subspace and coordinate
ambiguities in recovering the original key from one message direction.
The correspondence between that direction and its bit string is
specified by the keyed codebook.

\subsection{Removal and Transfer}\label{sec:transfer}

Key identifiability and removal resistance concern different objects:
the original key can remain ambiguous while the combined signal
direction is estimable. With the public carrier $a(t)=\sin(2\pi t)$, a recipient can estimate
the carrier-aligned component of the marked velocity by least-squares
regression onto $a(t)$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:carrier-removal}
\widehat r=\frac{\sum_i a(t_i)v_w(x_i,t_i)}{\sum_i a(t_i)^2},
\qquad v_\alpha(x,t)=v_w(x,t)-\alpha a(t)\widehat r.
\end{equation}
This is the same regression that underlies the detector
\eqref{eq:detection}, without the secret projection $P$ or the
codebook: the detector estimates the carrier-aligned component in the
keyed subspace, while \eqref{eq:carrier-removal} estimates it in the
full ambient space. The estimate therefore contains both the watermark
and any carrier-correlated background, and it requires no key, no
codebook, and no clean-model responses.
When the carrier is unknown, the recipient can instead regress onto a
candidate $b(t)$ and subtract $\alpha b(t)\widehat r_b$, replacing
$a$ by $b$ in~\eqref{eq:carrier-removal}. We compare matched sine
regression with the mismatched candidate $b(t)=\cos(2\pi t)$.
Full-backbone fine-tuning instead changes the marked field through
ordinary flow-matching updates. Section~\ref{sec:modification} studies
parameter modifications, and Section~\ref{sec:removal-transfer} evaluates
these two removal mechanisms.

Distillation transfers behavior through teacher
supervision~\cite{hinton2015distilling}. Velocity supervision directly fits the marked field, whereas sample-only supervision constrains only the terminal distribution and may retain part or all of the encoded response. We evaluate both interfaces using exact-message and bitwise recovery in Section~\ref{sec:removal-transfer}.

\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}

We evaluate the watermark on fidelity to the clean model, message
capacity, recovery reliability, clean-model false-positive rate,
robustness to utility-preserving modification, and verification cost.
Because existing methods modify different objects, cross-method
comparison is at the interface level and is summarized in
Appendix~\ref{app:comparison}.

Image experiments use MLP, RF-UNet, and SD3.5 DiT generators on MNIST,
CIFAR-10, CelebA-64, or image latents. Audio experiments use an MLP and
a Transformer with frozen Music2Latent representations~\cite{music2latent},
and Stable Audio 3 Small Music Base with its own codec~\cite{stableaudio3base}.
Audio data are six-second FMA-small recordings with the official
recording-level split~\cite{fmadataset}.

The main comparisons use five messages per setting and projection
dimension $k=32$: five-bit orthogonal encoding (O5), 32-bit hypercube
encoding (H32), and a five-bit hypercube comparison (H5).
Pretrained generators use LoRA~\cite{hu2022lora}; scratch models update
all weights. The image MLP uses encoding-specific embedding objectives.
Recovery and quality results are reported as mean $\pm$ population
standard deviation across five messages. Appendix~\ref{app:embedding-configurations}
gives model-specific optimization, query, and sampling protocols.

\subsection{Recovery and Quality Preservation}

Exact recovery requires reconstruction of the complete embedded bit
string; bit accuracy is the fraction of correctly decoded bits.
Clean-model FPR is the fraction of independently sampled uniform message
claims that match the clean model's decoded message.
The quality ratio is the marked model's feature distance to a reference
divided by the clean model's distance to the same reference, with a
ratio near one indicating preservation. Distances are Inception FID for
images and Fr\'echet distance in CLAP feature space for
audio~\cite{clapcheckpoint}; the reference is real images for MNIST and
CIFAR-10, base-model samples for CelebA-64 and SD3.5, and held-out FMA
recordings for audio. Table~\ref{tab:main} reports these metrics;
Appendix~\ref{app:fpr} gives the sampling procedure.

\subsection{Embedding and Verification Cost}\label{sec:embedding-cost}

Embedding is cheap. On CIFAR-10 RF-UNet, the five-bit watermarks recover

estimation can suppress the mark without identifying its original
key. Half-strength subtraction preserves complete recovery for both
encodings, whereas unit-strength subtraction gives near-chance bit accuracy.
In contrast, mismatched cosine subtraction preserves exact recovery
for both encodings. Sine and cosine are orthogonal under uniform-time
sampling, so the subtracted cosine component has zero population
correlation with the owner's sine carrier.

Velocity-supervised students retain both encodings completely.
Sample-only transfer is encoding-dependent: O5 remains fully
recoverable, while H32 retains high bit accuracy despite low
exact-message recovery. This residual information makes exact recovery
alone an incomplete measure of removal. For H32, bit decisions
correspond directly to signed axes; O5 bit accuracy instead depends on
the assigned labels of its orthogonal codewords. The five messages
reuse the original key-generation seed and are not independent key
replications.

These results place the method in a specific setting. It supports
provenance auditing of redistributed checkpoints, moderately compressed
or adapted copies, and velocity-supervised students. A hosted-model deployment that exposes velocity queries would allow the same auditing without releasing weights. It does not prevent removal by a recipient who knows the construction: matched-carrier subtraction and full-backbone fine-tuning both suppress recovery while leaving
generation quality at or below the marked teacher. Removal under
fine-tuning is not specific to this method, since fine-tuning removes
output-level and weight-level watermarks as well~\cite{hu2024unstable}; the construction-specific exposure here is carrier subtraction. These results follow from the access model rather than from the
embedding. An openly released checkpoint can be updated by any
recipient, and no mark carried by such a model can bound how far.
The removal results therefore measure what any mark on an open
checkpoint can promise. The appendix compares the conditions under which other methods are reported to persist or fail.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Conclusion}

Dynamics-level watermarking encodes a keyed message in the velocity
field of a flow matching model and recovers it through synchronous
demodulation. The formulation separates message encoding from the
architecture of the generator. Its analysis relates code geometry and
query count to message information, and bounds the distributional
change induced by the watermark under regularity assumptions.

Experiments demonstrate recovery and quality preservation across image
and audio generators, and characterize payload, embedding cost, and
verification cost. The modification and transfer studies delimit the
scope: attribution holds for redistributed checkpoints, moderately
adapted copies, and velocity-supervised students when velocity queries
are available, and fails under carrier subtraction or full-backbone
fine-tuning. Dynamics-level watermarking therefore serves as a
provenance signal for auditing released model copies, with persistence
determined by how the recipient modifies or transfers the generator.

Extending the update budget would require placing the mark where the
recipient's update does not act. SleeperMark pursues this by
disentangling the mark from the part of the model that downstream
fine-tuning updates; whether a dynamics-level construction can do the
same is open.

Code and experimental configurations are publicly available at:
\url{https://github.com/ShuchanWang/flow-matching-dynamics-watermarking}.

\section{Preservation Analysis}
\label{app:preservation}

We derive the drift bound used in Definition~\ref{def:admissible}, then
specialize it to the proposed carrier and compare exact preservation regimes.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Preliminaries}

Let $u : \R^D \times [0,1] \to \R^D$ be the clean velocity field and
$\varphi_t$ its flow map. Under the noise distribution $p_0$, the flow
induces a density $\rho(\cdot, t) = (\varphi_t)_\# p_0$ satisfying
\begin{equation}
\partial_t \rho + \nabla\!\cdot\!(\rho\,u) = 0,
\label{eq:ce-clean}
\end{equation}
with generative distribution $\rho(\cdot, 1) = (\varphi_1)_\# p_0$.
Write $v = u + \delta u$ for the watermarked velocity, and
$\varphi_t^w$, $\rho^w$ for the corresponding flow map and density.
For a perturbation of strength $\epsilon$, write the corresponding
density as
\begin{equation}
\rho^\epsilon(\cdot,t)
=\rho(\cdot,t)+\epsilon\,\xi(\cdot,t)+O(\epsilon^2),
\label{eq:density-expansion}
\end{equation}
where $\xi$ is the first-order density perturbation. For $0\le s\le t\le 1$, let $\mathcal{T}_{t\leftarrow s}$ denote the
transport operator associated with the homogeneous part of the
linearized continuity equation around the clean flow, as used in the
definition of $M_T$ in \eqref{eq:transport-amplification}. It
propagates a density perturbation from time $s$ to time $t$ under the
clean dynamics $u$. This operator controls transport of density perturbations
under the clean dynamics~\cite{diperna1989ordinary,ambrosio2004transport}.

The proposed perturbation is
\begin{equation}
\delta u(x,t) = \epsilon\,\alpha(t)\,P c_m,
\qquad \alpha(t) = \sin(2\pi t),
\label{eq:proposed}
\end{equation}
with $P \in \R^{D \times k}$ semi-orthogonal, $c_m$ a unit codeword,
and $\epsilon > 0$.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{First-Order Distribution Drift}

We first bound the drift for a general signal $s_{K,m}(x,t)$ that may
depend on the state $x$, then specialize to the proposed carrier
$s_{K,m}(x,t) = \alpha(t) P c_m$, which is constant in $x$.

\begin{theorem}[First-order drift bound]
\label{thm:drift}
Assume the regularity conditions on $u$ and $\rho$ under which the
continuity equation has a unique flow and the transport operator
$\mathcal{T}_{t \leftarrow s}$ is well defined, and assume that the
expansion \eqref{eq:density-expansion} holds in total variation. For $\delta u=\epsilon s_{K,m}$,
\begin{equation}
\bigl\|\rho^w(\cdot,1)-\rho(\cdot,1)\bigr\|_{\TV}
\le \epsilon M_T\int_0^1
\bigl\|\nabla\!\cdot(\rho(\cdot,r)s_{K,m}(\cdot,r))\bigr\|_{\TV}\,dr
+O(\epsilon^2).
\label{eq:general-drift-bound}
\end{equation}
For $s_{K,m}(x,t)=\alpha(t)q$, $q=P c_m$, this specializes to
\begin{equation}
\bigl\|\rho^w(\cdot,1) - \rho(\cdot,1)\bigr\|_{\TV}
\;\le\;
\epsilon\,M_T
\int_0^1 |\alpha(r)|\,\|q^\top\nabla\rho(\cdot,r)\|_{\TV}\,dr
\,+\,O(\epsilon^2).
\label{eq:drift-bound}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $\rho^\epsilon=\rho^w$ be the density induced by
$u+\epsilon s_{K,m}$. In the expansion
\eqref{eq:density-expansion}, $\xi(\cdot,0)=0$ because the clean and
perturbed flows use the same initial distribution. Substituting
\eqref{eq:density-expansion} into the
continuity equation
\begin{equation}
\partial_t\rho^\epsilon
+\nabla\!\cdot\!\left(\rho^\epsilon(u+\epsilon s_{K,m})\right)=0
\end{equation}
and collecting first-order terms in $\epsilon$ gives
\begin{equation}
\partial_t\xi+\nabla\!\cdot(\xi u)
=
-\nabla\!\cdot(\rho s_{K,m}).
\label{eq:linearized-continuity}
\end{equation}
Duhamel's principle yields
\begin{equation}
\xi(\cdot,1)
=
-\int_0^1
\mathcal{T}_{1\leftarrow r}\!\left[
\nabla\!\cdot(\rho(\cdot,r)s_{K,m}(\cdot,r))
\right]\,dr .
\end{equation}
Therefore
\begin{equation}
\|\rho^\epsilon(\cdot,1)-\rho(\cdot,1)\|_{\TV}
\le
\epsilon\|\xi(\cdot,1)\|_{\TV}+O(\epsilon^2),
\end{equation}
and the triangle inequality gives
\begin{equation}
\|\xi(\cdot,1)\|_{\TV}
\le
\int_0^1
\bigl\|\mathcal{T}_{1\leftarrow r}[\nabla\!\cdot(\rho s_{K,m})(\cdot,r)]\bigr\|_{\TV}\,dr .
\end{equation}
The definition of $M_T$ gives \eqref{eq:general-drift-bound}. For the
proposed signal, $\nabla\!\cdot(\rho s_{K,m})=\alpha(r)q^\top\nabla\rho$,
which gives \eqref{eq:drift-bound}.
\end{proof}

The terminal-TV requirement \eqref{eq:admissible-drift} is satisfied
when the linear term in \eqref{eq:general-drift-bound} plus its
higher-order $O(\epsilon^2)$ contribution is at most
$\delta_{\mathrm{dist}}$.

\begin{corollary}[Zero mean removes the time-invariant drift component]
\label{cor:zero-mean-gap}
Let
\[
G_q(r)=
\mathcal{T}_{1\leftarrow r}\!\left[
q^\top\nabla\rho(\cdot,r)
\right].
\]
If $\int_0^1\alpha(r)\,dr=0$, then for any signed measure $\bar G$,
\begin{equation}
\xi(\cdot,1)
=
-\int_0^1 \alpha(r)\,\bigl(G_q(r)-\bar G\bigr)\,dr .
\label{eq:zero-mean-residual}
\end{equation}
Consequently, the zero-mean carrier cancels the time-invariant component
of the first-order forcing. If, in addition, $G_q$ is $L_q$-Lipschitz in
total variation, then choosing $\bar G=G_q(1/2)$ and
$\alpha(r)=\sin(2\pi r)$ gives
\begin{equation}
\|\xi(\cdot,1)\|_{\TV}
\le
\frac{L_q}{2\pi}.
\label{eq:zero-mean-lipschitz}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
From the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:drift}, the first-order density
perturbation at time $1$ is
\begin{equation}
\xi(\cdot,1)
=
-\int_0^1 \alpha(r)\,
G_q(r)\,dr .
\end{equation}
Because $\int_0^1\alpha(r)\,dr=0$,
$\int_0^1\alpha(r)\bar G\,dr=0$ for any $\bar G$, which proves
\eqref{eq:zero-mean-residual}. If
$\|G_q(r)-G_q(s)\|_{\TV}\le L_q|r-s|$, then
\[
\|\xi(\cdot,1)\|_{\TV}
\le
L_q\int_0^1 |\sin(2\pi r)|\,|r-1/2|\,dr
= \frac{L_q}{2\pi},
\]
which proves \eqref{eq:zero-mean-lipschitz}.
\end{proof}

Zero mean cancels the time-invariant forcing component. Under the
Lipschitz assumption, terminal distortion is at most
$\epsilon L_q/(2\pi)+O(\epsilon^2)$.

\section{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:capacity}}
\label{app:capacity_proof}

We derive the additive Gaussian approximation for the demodulated
signature, then prove the information bound for a finite codebook
under that model.

\subsection{Step 1: Approximate Effective Channel}

Given a watermarked model $v_{\theta,K,m}$, we query it at $N$ random
points $\{(x_i, t_i)\}_{i=1}^N$ drawn i.i.d.~from a sampling distribution
$p(x,t)$ and compute the signature:
\begin{equation}
\hat{s} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \sin(2\pi t_i) \cdot P^T v_{\theta,K,m}(x_i, t_i).
\end{equation}

Decompose the velocity field as
$v_{\theta,K,m} = v_\theta + \eta_0 + \epsilon \sin(2\pi t) P c_m$,
where $v_\theta$ is the learned flow, $\eta_0$ represents background
variation from training stochasticity and model approximation error, and
$\epsilon \sin(2\pi t) P c_m$ is the watermark signal. Substituting:

\begin{align}
\hat{s} &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \sin(2\pi t_i) P^T [v_\theta(x_i,t_i) + \eta_0(x_i,t_i)] \\
        &\quad + \frac{\epsilon}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \sin^2(2\pi t_i) P^T P c_m.
\end{align}

Since $P^T P = I_k$ by construction, the second term simplifies to:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\epsilon}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \sin^2(2\pi t_i) \cdot c_m.
\end{equation}

Define the background term for each query:
\begin{equation}
z_i := \sin(2\pi t_i) P^T [v_\theta(x_i, t_i) + \eta_0(x_i, t_i)] \in \mathbb{R}^k.
\end{equation}

We introduce the following assumptions.

\begin{assumption}[Uncorrelated carrier and background]\label{ass:uncorrelated}
The temporal carrier $\sin(2\pi t)$ is uncorrelated with the background
velocity field:
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}_{x,t}[\sin(2\pi t) \cdot P^T(v_\theta(x,t) + \eta_0(x,t))] = 0.
\end{equation}
\end{assumption}

This assumption concerns the joint dependence of the carrier and the
time-varying background velocity.

\begin{assumption}[Independent queries]\label{ass:iid}
The query points $\{(x_i, t_i)\}_{i=1}^N$ are drawn i.i.d.~from a
distribution $p(x,t)$ under which the background terms $z_i$ have finite
second moments.
\end{assumption}

Queries are sampled independently; finite second moments remain a
moment assumption. Under these conditions, the $z_i$ are i.i.d.~random vectors
with mean $\mathbb{E}[z_i] = \mathbf{0}$ (by Assumption~\ref{ass:uncorrelated})
and covariance matrix
\begin{equation}
\Sigma := \text{Cov}(z_i) = \mathbb{E}[z_i z_i^T] \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times k}.
\end{equation}

For the watermark term, we have:
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[\sin^2(2\pi t)] = \int_0^1 \sin^2(2\pi t) dt = \frac{1}{2}.
\end{equation}
By the Law of Large Numbers,
$\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \sin^2(2\pi t_i) \to \frac{1}{2}$ as $N \to \infty$.

For the background term, we apply the multivariate Central Limit Theorem
(CLT). Let $\xi := \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N z_i$. Under
Assumptions~\ref{ass:uncorrelated} and~\ref{ass:iid}, as $N \to \infty$:
\begin{equation}
\sqrt{N} \xi \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma),
\end{equation}
where $\xrightarrow{d}$ denotes convergence in distribution. Equivalently,
for large $N$:
\begin{equation}
\xi \approx \mathcal{N}\!\left(0, \frac{\Sigma}{N}\right).
\end{equation}

\begin{assumption}[Gaussian approximation for finite $N$]\label{ass:gaussian}
For the query counts $N$ used in practice, the distribution of $\xi$ is
well-approximated by $\mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma/N)$.
\end{assumption}

While the CLT guarantees asymptotic normality under the stated moment
condition, its accuracy at finite $N$ depends on the distribution of
$z_i$; Assumption~\ref{ass:gaussian} specifies the approximation used
by the finite-query analysis.

The empirical average of $\sin^2(2\pi t_i)$ also fluctuates around
$1/2$ at finite $N$. Neglecting the fluctuation
and approximating the background average by a Gaussian gives the
surrogate channel used in Theorem~\ref{thm:capacity}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:channel_final}
\boxed{\hat{s} = \frac{\epsilon}{2} c_m + \xi, \quad \xi \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(0, \frac{\Sigma}{N}\right).}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Step 2: The Noise Covariance}

The noise covariance $\Sigma$ captures the background velocity variation
in the projected $k$-dimensional space. Let
$b(x,t) := \sin(2\pi t)[v_\theta(x,t) + \eta_0(x,t)] \in \mathbb{R}^D$.
Then:
\begin{equation}
\Sigma = P^T \, \mathbb{E}_{x,t}[b(x,t) b(x,t)^T] \, P.
\end{equation}

\begin{assumption}[Invertible covariance]\label{ass:invertible}
The projected covariance $\Sigma$ is invertible.
\end{assumption}

Invertibility requires background variation along every projected
direction and makes the covariance whitening below well defined.

\subsection{Step 3: Finite-Codebook Information Bound}

Let $a=\epsilon/2$, $Z\sim\mathcal N(0,\Sigma/N)$, and
$Y=a c_M+Z$. For the finite message distribution, put
$Q=\operatorname{Cov}(c_M)$. Since $Z$ is independent of $M$,
$\operatorname{Cov}(Y)=a^2Q+\Sigma/N$. Gaussian vectors maximize
differential entropy at fixed covariance~\cite{cover1999elements}; hence
\begin{align}
I(M;Y)
&=h(Y)-h(Z)\\
&\leq\frac12\log\frac{\det(a^2Q+\Sigma/N)}{\det(\Sigma/N)}\\
&=\frac12\log\det\!\left(I_k+
\frac{N\epsilon^2}{4}\Sigma^{-1/2}Q\Sigma^{-1/2}\right).
\end{align}
Also $I(M;Y)\leq H(M)$, proving the first claim. Since every
codeword has unit norm,
$\operatorname{tr}Q=1-\|\mathbb E[c_M]\|^2\leq1$.
Maximizing the Gaussian expression over all positive semidefinite
$Q$ with trace at most one gives the unit-power Gaussian relaxation.
The selected codebook determines its own covariance $Q$ within this
optimization domain.

\subsection{Scaling Analysis}\label{app:hypercube-scaling}

Let $\nu_1,\ldots,\nu_k$ be the eigenvalues of
$\Sigma^{-1/2}Q\Sigma^{-1/2}$. The Gaussian upper bound is
\[
\frac12\sum_j\log(1+N\epsilon^2\nu_j/4).
\]
For fixed positive definite $\Sigma$ and fixed $Q$, its large-$N$
coefficient of $\log N$ is $\operatorname{rank}(Q)/2$, the number of codebook
directions carrying message variation. This is a property of the relaxed
Gaussian expression: the finite-alphabet information $I(M;Y)$ is
always capped by $H(M)$ and approaches that cap as the message
distributions become distinguishable. At finite $N$, the nonzero
$\nu_j$ govern how quickly the bound rises. Correlations in projected
velocity affect these values, but covariance alone does not establish
the reliability of a particular decoder.

For uniform hypercube messages on $L$ orthonormal axes,
$Q=L^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^Le_je_j^T$ and $H(M)=L\log 2$. If additionally
$\Sigma=\sigma^2 I_k$, sign decoding has independent bit-error
probability $\Phi(-\epsilon\sqrt{N}/(2\sigma\sqrt L))$ under the
surrogate, where $\Phi$ is the standard-normal CDF. Exact-message
recovery is then $(1-p_{\rm bit})^L$, giving
\eqref{eq:hypercube-exact-recovery}. The per-bit success probability
decreases with $L$ and lies below one; increasing its exponent also
decreases exact recovery. The prediction holds at fixed $N$,
$\epsilon$, and $\sigma$ even though total codeword energy remains one.

\subsection{Relationship to Experiments}

The channel model relates the chosen codebook, noise covariance, and
query count to recoverable message information. For the hypercube,
the bit-error expression above makes the role of payload explicit:
fixed total signal energy gives less energy to each bit as $L$ grows,
and complete recovery requires all bit decisions to succeed.
Table~\ref{tab:payload-main} exhibits the resulting effective payload
limit at fixed projection dimension and query count. Changing the
projection dimension provides an additional way to adjust the
signaling space.


\section{Evaluation and Embedding Protocols}\label{app:embedding-configurations}

\subsection{Recovery and Quality}

For Table~\ref{tab:main}, recovery uses five target messages and 20 trials per message, except
CIFAR-10 H32, which uses 40 trials. Image MLP, RF-UNet, and audio trials
use 4096 velocity queries; SD3.5 trials use 256. Image MLP queries are
pure noise. RF-UNet, SD3.5, and audio queries use the interpolation
distributions associated with their data or latent pools. Recovery is
therefore compared under model-specific query protocols, not equal
query cost.

Image MLP and RF-UNet quality use 500 samples with the same Inception
feature extractor and preprocessing. SD3.5 uses 30 samples and the
standard descending-noise SD3 sampler. Scratch audio uses 500 clips;
Stable Audio uses 32. Image and audio distances use their respective
feature spaces, and ratios are interpreted relative to each setting's
clean model and reference distribution. The SD3.5 and Stable Audio
quality estimates have especially small sample counts.
The high-resolution SD3.5 figure uses 40 sampling steps and guidance
scale 4.5; its matched clean and marked seeds are fixed before generation.

\subsection{Clean-Model FPR}\label{app:fpr}

For MLP FPR, 10,000 trials each use a new key, 4096 fresh pure-noise
queries, and an independent uniform message claim. Velocity queries are
shared across encodings. The zero-match H32 result has a one-sided
95\% upper bound of $0.030\%$. These trials use one clean checkpoint.

Other generators use 20 clean decoded outputs under one saved key and
one million independent uniform claims. Query distributions and query counts
match recovery evaluation; audio and SD3.5 H32 reuse recorded clean
decodes. The million trials sample claims, not fresh model responses.
For zero matches, the one-sided 95\% upper bound is $0.000300\%$ for
this conditional claim-sampling experiment. These counts do not
estimate acceptance of a chosen fixed target across independent models.

\subsection{Model-Specific Optimization}

The image MLPs are trained from scratch for 5000 epochs, or 115,000 updates
at batch size 512 on 12,000 images, followed by 50 updates of velocity
distillation and decoding-margin optimization. SD3.5 H32 uses 700
optimizer updates with rank-16 LoRA and projected-residual and
bit-margin losses. Training-time bias compensation is applied in the
final 150 updates, with nominal strength 3.0 in the final objective.
Verification uses raw marked velocities and the saved key.

\subsection{Audio Implementation}\label{app:audio-results}

Audio experiments use six-second clips from FMA-small~\cite{fmadataset}.
Training and held-out recordings follow the official split. The scratch
MLP and Transformer use a frozen Music2Latent codec~\cite{music2latent},
with 2,000 training clips and 500 held-out clips. Clean training uses 5,000
updates; embedding uses 1,000 updates with batch size 32 and initial
learning rate $10^{-4}$.

Stable Audio 3 Small Music Base is the rectified-flow
checkpoint~\cite{stableaudio3base}. Its own frozen codec produces
$256\times66$ latents per clip; the projection acts on all 16,896
flattened coordinates. The experiment uses 128 training and 32 held-out
recordings, the fixed prompt \textit{An instrumental music recording},
and rank-16, alpha-16 LoRA on linear layers (10,277,184 trainable
parameters). The backbone's descending noise-time coordinate is
converted to forward flow time by $v(x,t)=-u(x,1-t)$.
Embedding uses batch size one, initial learning rate $10^{-4}$,
AdamW, cosine decay, and nominal strength 1.5.

Stable Audio O5 uses 100 updates per message; four runs use a 100-update
cosine schedule and one uses the first 100 updates of a 500-update
schedule. H32 uses 400 total updates, with bias-compensated residual
fitting and a bit-margin loss in the final 100. Continuations restart
cosine decay at $10^{-4}$.

Bias compensation estimates the clean demodulated projection $b_K$
from 4096 training-pool queries. With $\alpha(t)=\sin(2\pi t)$ and
nominal strength $\varepsilon=1.5$, the projected residual target is
$\alpha(t)(\varepsilon c_m-2b_K)$, using
$\mathbb E[\alpha(t)^2]=1/2$. The additional unit-weight penalty is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{bit}}=\frac{1}{BL}\sum_{i=1}^B\sum_{j=1}^L
\left[\frac{\varepsilon\alpha(t_i)^2}{\sqrt L}
-s_j\alpha(t_i)(P^\top v_\theta(x_i,t_i))_j\right]_+,
\qquad s_j=2m_j-1,
\end{equation}
where $B$ is batch size and $[a]_+=\max(a,0)$. Compensation changes the
target direction and norm, so nominal strength alone does not specify
the effective perturbation. The residual projection weight is 10 and
the carrier-correlation weight is one. Clean-model information is used
only for embedding; recovery uses raw marked velocities and the key.
Bias estimation and recovery use disjoint training and held-out pools.

The codec encodes padded 44.1\,kHz inputs. Decoded clips are truncated
to six seconds and averaged to mono for quality evaluation. Generation
uses 100 forward-Euler steps without classifier-free guidance
amplification.

\section{Comparison with Existing Methods}\label{app:comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:comparison} lists representative methods by what the
signal is attached to, what the verifier must access, and the weakness
that follows from the attachment. Each weakness is a consequence of
where the signal lives: a signal in the sampling path is lost when the
path changes, a signal in the weights requires the weights and is lost
when they are updated, and a signal in a trigger is defined only on the
trigger set. The construction here attaches the signal to the velocity
field and is verified from velocity queries alone.

\begin{table}[!htbp]
\centering
\caption{Signal attachment, verification access, and principal
weakness of representative watermarking interfaces.}
\label{tab:comparison}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
\toprule
Method & Attached to & Verification & Weakness \\
\midrule
Tree-Ring~\cite{wen2023tree}
  & Sampling noise & Images + inversion & Sampling path \\
Stable Signature~\cite{fernandez2023stable}
  & Image decoder & Images + extractor & Decoder fine-tuning~\cite{hu2024unstable} \\
Weight embedding~\cite{uchida2017embedding}
  & Selected weights & Model weights & Weight updates \\

\paragraph{Output-level methods.}
Tree-Ring and Stable Signature verify from generated images, with no
need for weights or a special interface. That attachment is also their
weakness. Tree-Ring depends on the noise-to-image path, so an operation
that breaks the correspondence suppresses
detection~\cite{lin2025crack}. Stable Signature depends on the decoder,
so decoder fine-tuning removes it while preserving image
quality~\cite{hu2024unstable}. Both are tied to a pipeline stage that
can be replaced without replacing the generator.

\paragraph{Weight-level methods.}
Weight embedding is precise and cheap when the weights are available,
and it requires white-box access, which excludes any audit of a hosted
model or a released binary. It is tied to a parameterization: any
procedure that reinitializes or substantially updates the weights
removes the mark. The reported survival under fine-tuning and pruning
concerns a subset of parameters and a bounded pruning level, not
full-weight optimization~\cite{uchida2017embedding}.

\paragraph{Trigger-based methods.}
Trigger-based methods verify from designated input--response pairs,
which permits black-box verification on a narrow input set. That
narrowness is the weakness: the mark is defined on the trigger set, and
a verifier who cannot query those inputs cannot verify it.

\paragraph{Latent and persistence-aware methods.}
SleeperMark attaches a latent residual to a secret prompt trigger and
is designed to survive downstream fine-tuning. It is the strongest
persistence result in this comparison, and the persistence is bounded
by the fine-tuning family it was trained against: under general
fine-tuning, including full-weight optimization, the mark
degrades~\cite{wang2025sleepermark}. Verification is also conditioned
on the trigger prompt.

\paragraph{This construction.}
The signal is attached to the velocity field and verified by querying
it, without inspecting weights or generating samples. It survives
compression and velocity-supervised distillation. Its weakness is shared with every method in the table: enough
full-parameter updates replace the field and remove the mark. Carrier subtraction behaves differently depending on what the adversary
knows. With the published carrier, the regression estimates the
watermark amplitude plus the carrier-aligned component of the clean
field, and subtracting it cancels the watermark. With an unknown carrier, the
adversary must estimate the carrier itself, and the clean field's
carrier-aligned component is indistinguishable from it in the response,
so the carrier estimate is inexact and the subtraction is inexact as
well.
\paragraph{Choice.}
The methods are complementary rather than ordered. If the verifier has
only generated images, the output-level methods apply and this one does
not. If the verifier has weights, weight embedding applies and is more
precise than a query-based check. If the verifier has velocity access
and the copy has undergone compression or velocity-supervised
distillation, this construction applies. No method in the table
survives an unbounded number of full-parameter updates.
