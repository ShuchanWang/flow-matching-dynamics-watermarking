\title{Dynamics-Level Watermarking of Flow Matching Models with Random Codes}
\author{Shuchan Wang}
\date{\today}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We introduce a dynamics-level approach to watermarking generative models.
Rather than embedding signals into model weights or outputs, we embed the
watermark directly into the learned continuous dynamics---the velocity
field of a flow matching model. We formulate this as random coding over a
continuous channel: a key-dependent perturbation is added during training,
and the message is recovered at detection time from black-box velocity
queries. The
perturbation is designed to leave the generated distribution unchanged.
Experiments on MNIST and CIFAR-10 across different architectures confirm
reliable message recovery, preserved generation quality, and poor
message recovery under independently sampled wrong keys.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Background: Flow Matching Generative Models}

Flow matching~\cite{lipmanflow,liuflow} has emerged as a powerful framework
for training continuous normalizing flows. Given samples $x_1 \sim
q_{\text{data}}$ from a data distribution and $x_0 \sim p_{\text{noise}}$
from a simple base distribution, flow matching learns a time-dependent
velocity field $v_\theta(x,t)$ that transports $p_{\text{noise}}$ to
$q_{\text{data}}$ along the trajectory
\begin{equation}
\frac{dx_t}{dt} = v_\theta(x_t, t), \quad t \in [0,1].
\end{equation}
The model is trained by regressing $v_\theta$ against a conditional
velocity field $u(x_t|x_0,x_1)$ defined along interpolating paths
$x_t = (1-t)x_0 + t x_1$. At convergence, integrating the ODE from
$x_0 \sim p_{\text{noise}}$ produces samples $x_1 \sim q_{\text{data}}$.

The key object learned by flow matching is the velocity field
$v_\theta: \mathbb{R}^d \times [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}^d$, a continuous
function that completely characterizes the generative process. Training
such a model requires substantial computational resources, making the
resulting velocity field valuable intellectual property in need of
protection against unauthorized use.

\subsection{Requirements for Generative Model Watermarking}

A watermarking scheme for a generative model should satisfy the following
properties. The watermark should be embedded in the model itself, so that
it persists across different generated outputs and cannot be circumvented
simply by bypassing a post-processing step (model-level embedding).
Detection should require velocity-query access to the model, without
access to weights or training data. This permits verification through
an API that exposes velocity evaluations, but not through an API that
returns only generated images (black-box verifiability under the stated
query interface). Watermarked models should generate samples that are
statistically indistinguishable from those of unwatermarked models, with
no visible artifacts or degradation in fidelity (quality preservation).
Detection and message recovery should require knowledge of a secret key;
without the key, an attacker should be unable to recover the embedded
message beyond chance level (keyed security). Finally, the scheme
should support encoding a multi-bit message, enabling applications beyond
binary ownership verification, including owner identification, model
versioning, customer-specific serialization, and traitor tracing across
distributed copies (multi-bit capacity).
This last setting is especially natural for scaling: independent model
copies assigned to different users provide independent verification
channels, whereas different coordinates inside a single velocity field
can remain correlated.

\subsection{Limitations of Existing Approaches}

We categorize existing watermarking methods by what they watermark.
Sampling-level methods such as Tree-Ring modify the latent noise used
to generate images~\cite{wen2023tree}. Decoder-level methods such as
Stable Signature fine-tune the generator's image decoder so its outputs
carry a detectable signal~\cite{fernandez2023stable}. Both are verified
from images; their respective attack surfaces are the sampling process
and the decoder, rather than direct access to the velocity field.
Weight-level methods embed bits into model
parameters~\cite{uchida2017embedding}. While model-level, they require
white-box access and are fragile to fine-tuning, pruning, or
quantization. Behavior-level methods use backdoor triggers---specific inputs that
produce predetermined outputs~\cite{adi2018turning,chen2019blackmarks,fei2025high}.
These methods operate at the model level and support black-box
verification. However, trigger-based approaches can introduce visible
artifacts or statistical irregularities, and often lack strong
cryptographic keying: once a trigger is discovered, any party can verify
the watermark. Recent work extends this to multi-bit payloads via channel
coding~\cite{fei2025high}.

Table~\ref{tab:comparison} in Appendix~\ref{app:comparison} summarizes how
existing paradigms compare against the five desiderata. To our knowledge,
no prior work embeds the watermark directly into the learned continuous
dynamics of the generative process itself. Existing black-box multi-bit
methods instead rely on trigger behaviors, output statistics, or
parameter-space embeddings, rather than perturbations of the transport
dynamics.

\subsection{The Gap: Dynamics-Level Watermarking}

In flow matching, the velocity field $v_\theta(x,t)$ is a continuous
function that defines the generative dynamics. This perspective reveals an underexplored opportunity: the velocity field can be viewed as a high-dimensional continuous channel into which information may be
embedded through structured perturbations, while preserving the overall
behavior of the model.

This gap motivates our core question:
\begin{quote}
\emph{Can we embed a verifiable, keyed, multi-bit message directly into
the learned velocity field of a flow matching model, such that (1) the
watermark is detectable through black-box velocity queries, (2)
generation quality is preserved, and (3) an independent wrong key fails
to recover the message?}
\end{quote}

\subsection{Our Approach and Contributions}

Our specific contributions are as follows. First, we formulate
watermarking directly at the level of the learned dynamics, embedding the
message as a key-dependent perturbation of the continuous velocity field
rather than into weights or outputs (model-level embedding). Second, we
use a zero-mean temporal carrier that avoids a persistent drift direction
and enables synchronous demodulation at detection time; sample quality is
evaluated empirically through FID (quality preservation). Third, the watermark is keyed by a secret projection matrix and a
codebook; independently sampled wrong keys fail to recover the tested
messages, while adaptive key attacks remain open. Fourth, the scheme supports multi-bit messages via the codebook construction, enabling identification beyond binary verification (multi-bit capacity).
Fifth, we validate the method on MNIST, CIFAR-10, CelebA-64, and
SD3.5 latent models, achieving $100\%$ five-bit message recovery in the
reported cross-model settings and FID ratios near their respective clean
references
(empirical validation). These properties make dynamics-level watermarking
a potential foundation for applications such as model ownership
verification, traitor tracing, and regulatory compliance, though we leave
their deployment-scale evaluation to future work.

\section{Watermarking as Random Coding over Transport Dynamics}

In this section, we formalize our approach. We first describe the
information-theoretic motivation, then detail the construction of keys,
codes, and the time-modulated watermark, followed by the training and
detection procedures.

\subsection{Information-Theoretic Motivation}

From an information-theoretic perspective, our approach views the velocity
field as a continuous additive channel. Let $v_\theta(x,t)$ be the clean
velocity field and consider a watermarked version:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vwm}
v_{\theta,K,m}(x,t) = v_\theta(x,t) + \eta(x,t) + \epsilon \cdot s_{K,m}(x,t),
\end{equation}
where $\eta(x,t)$ represents background variation from training
stochasticity and $s_{K,m}(x,t)$ is a key-dependent signal encoding
message $m$.

The detection problem becomes: given $N$ noisy observations of
$v_{\theta,K,m}(x_i,t_i)$, estimate $\hat{m}$ with high confidence.
This is a random coding problem over a continuous channel---a classical
setup in information theory \cite{cover1999elements} that guarantees
reliable decoding when the codebook is appropriately constructed and the
number of observations is sufficient.

\subsection{Message and Key: Framework}

Equation~\eqref{eq:vwm} reduces the watermarking problem to the
construction of the signal $s_{K,m}(x,t)$. We first give a general
formulation.

Let $m \in \mathcal{M}$ be a message from a set of
$|\mathcal{M}| := 2^L$ possible messages, and let $K$ be a secret key.
The key defines a codebook through a mapping
\begin{equation}
\phi_K : \mathcal{M} \to \mathcal{H},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{H}$ is a function space over $(x,t)$. The codeword for
message $m$ is $s_{K,m}(x,t) := \phi_K(m)$. We use the
$L^2$ norm over the query distribution on $(x,t)$ for $\mathcal H$.

The design of $s_{K,m}$ must balance three considerations: (i) detectability---the signal must be recoverable from noisy observations
through a known detection procedure; (ii) quality preservation---the
signal should avoid a persistent velocity bias and should not measurably
degrade the generated distribution; and (iii) security---without
knowledge of the key $K$, the watermark must be unrecoverable beyond chance level.

These considerations are formalized as the following three requirements
on the codebook.

\begin{definition}[Admissible codebook]\label{def:admissible}
A codebook $\{s_{K,m}\}_{m\in\mathcal{M}}$ is called \textbf{admissible}
if it satisfies the following conditions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Zero-mean over time.} For all $m \in \mathcal{M}$ and
all $x$,
\begin{equation}
\int_0^1 s_{K,m}(x,t) \, dt = 0.
\end{equation}
\item \textbf{Message separation.} Distinct messages have distinct
codewords, with minimum distance
\begin{equation}
\Delta_K:=\min_{m\ne m'}\|s_{K,m}-s_{K,m'}\|_{\mathcal H}>0.
\end{equation}
\item \textbf{Energy normalization.} There exists a constant $C > 0$
such that for all $m \in \mathcal{M}$,
\begin{equation}
\|s_{K,m}\|^2 \approx C.
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Condition~1 centers the temporal carrier: it removes the DC component of
the watermark, avoids a persistent drift direction, and makes the
detector recover the signal by demodulating with the known carrier.
Condition~2 makes messages distinguishable without noise; its minimum
distance influences finite-query decoding. Pairwise orthogonality is
one stronger way to separate codewords, not a requirement for every
code. Condition~3 gives all messages comparable signal energy. These
conditions are design
principles that guide our construction; they are neither necessary for
watermarking in general nor sufficient to guarantee empirical success.
We validate the practical performance of our construction in
Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.

\subsection{Concrete Construction}\label{sec:concrete}

We first present the pairwise-orthogonal construction satisfying
Definition~\ref{def:admissible}. Normalized random-vector and hypercube
codebooks are other keyed realizations of the same random-coding
framework, with different separation geometries; see
Remark~\ref{rem:alternatives} and Appendix~\ref{app:payload}.

The secret key consists of two components, $K = (P, C)$. Here
$P \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times k}$ is a semi-orthogonal projection matrix
satisfying $P^T P = I_k$, generated by QR decomposition of a random matrix
seeded by $K$. This maps from the $D$-dimensional velocity space to a
$k$-dimensional latent code space. The component
$C = \{c_m\}_{m \in \mathcal{M}}$ is a codebook of $|\mathcal{M}| = 2^L$
unit vectors in $\mathbb{R}^k$ such that each message maps to a distinct
codeword. When $2^L\leq k$, QR decomposition yields pairwise orthogonal
codewords. For $2^L>k$, the explicit-codebook experiments use normalized
independently sampled vectors, which are not pairwise orthogonal. The
implicit hypercube uses $L$ orthonormal code-space axes whose ambient
velocity directions $Pe_j$ are randomized by the same secret projection
$P$. Thus all three constructions are keyed random codes, although
their codeword geometries differ. The hypercube construction is
specified in Appendix~\ref{app:payload}.

Throughout the paper, let
\begin{equation}
\alpha(t) = \sin(2\pi t)
\end{equation}
denote the scalar temporal carrier. We define the watermark signal for
message $m$ as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:watermark_signal}
s_{K,m}(x,t) = \alpha(t) \cdot P c_m.
\end{equation}
The signal separates naturally into a spatial component $P c_m$, which
embeds the codeword into the velocity space, and a temporal carrier
$\alpha(t)$. The zero mean of $\alpha$ removes any constant velocity
offset, while its known phase lets the detector use matched filtering
through synchronous demodulation. It is therefore a detection-centering
choice, not by itself a proof of distribution preservation. The following
theorem verifies that this construction
satisfies all requirements.

\begin{theorem}[Verification of design properties]\label{thm:properties}
The construction~\eqref{eq:watermark_signal} satisfies the three conditions
of Definition~\ref{def:admissible}.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
We verify each condition.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Zero-mean over time:}
$\int_0^1 s_{K,m}(x,t) dt = P c_m \int_0^1 \alpha(t) dt = 0$.
\item \textbf{Message separation:}
Since $P^T P=I_k$ and the codewords are orthonormal,
$\|s_{K,m}-s_{K,m'}\|_{\mathcal H}^2
=\mathbb E_t[\alpha(t)^2]\|P(c_m-c_{m'})\|_2^2=1$
for distinct messages; hence $\Delta_K=1$.
\item \textbf{Energy normalization:}
$\|s_{K,m}\|^2 = \frac{1}{2} \|P c_m\|^2 = \frac{1}{2}$ for all $m$,
since $P$ preserves norms and $\|c_m\| = 1$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:alternatives}
This construction is one instance of a broader class. Alternative choices
include different carrier functions (e.g., $\cos(2\pi t)$, $\sin(4\pi t)$,
wavelet-based carriers), learned projections instead of random $P$, or
sparse codes instead of orthogonal codes. Preliminary
experiments with frequency- and time-division multiplexing did not improve
capacity over the single-carrier design (see
Appendix~\ref{app:multiplexing}); we leave a systematic investigation of
multi-carrier schemes to future work.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Watermarked Training}

We now describe how the watermark is embedded during flow matching
training. Given a training pair $(x_0, x_1)$ and interpolated state
$x_t = (1-t)x_0 + t x_1$, the standard flow matching target is
$u = x_1 - x_0$. We modify this to include the watermark:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{v}(x_t, t) = u + \epsilon_0 \cdot s_{K,m}(x_t, t)
= u + \epsilon_0 \sin(2\pi t) \cdot P c_m,
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon_0 > 0$ is the nominal perturbation amplitude applied
during training. The effective watermark strength $\epsilon$ in \eqref{eq:vwm} may differ from $\epsilon_0$,
because the $\mathcal{L}_{\text{wm}}$ term in \eqref{eq:loss} provides an additional
gradient that amplifies the effective
$\epsilon$.

The watermarked model $v_\theta$ is optimized with a combined objective:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:loss}
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{x,t}\left[\|v_\theta(x,t)
- \tilde{v}(x,t)\|^2\right]}_{\mathcal{L}_{\text{vel}}}
+ \lambda \cdot \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{x,t}\left[-\sin(2\pi t) \cdot
\langle P^T v_\theta(x,t), c_m \rangle\right]}_{\mathcal{L}_{\text{wm}}},
\end{equation}
where $\lambda > 0$ balances the two terms. The first term
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{vel}}$ ensures the model learns the watermarked
velocity field. The second term $\mathcal{L}_{\text{wm}}$ directly rewards the model for producing velocity vectors whose demodulated projection aligns with the
target codeword $c_m$ (see Section~\ref{sec:detection}). The following proposition shows how these two terms combine to
determine the effective watermark strength.

\begin{proposition}[Optimal predictor under the watermark objective]
\label{prop:optimal_predictor}
Assume expectations are finite and optimization is performed over all
square-integrable vector fields
$v:\mathbb{R}^D\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^D$.
Then the unique population minimizer of~\eqref{eq:loss} is
\begin{equation}
v^\star(x,t)
=
u(x,t)
+
\left(\epsilon_0+\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)
\sin(2\pi t)\,Pc_m.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since the objective is separable over $(x,t)$, it suffices to minimize the
pointwise integrand
\begin{equation}
\ell(v)
=
\|v-\tilde v\|^2
-
\lambda \sin(2\pi t)\langle P^T v,c_m\rangle.
\end{equation}

Using the identity
\[
\langle P^T v,c_m\rangle
=
\langle v,Pc_m\rangle,
\]
the gradient with respect to $v$ is
\begin{equation}
\nabla_v \ell(v)
=
2(v-\tilde v)
-
\lambda \sin(2\pi t)Pc_m.
\end{equation}

Setting the gradient to zero yields
\begin{equation}
v^\star
=
\tilde v
+
\frac{\lambda}{2}\sin(2\pi t)Pc_m.
\end{equation}

Substituting
\[
\tilde v
=
u+\epsilon_0 \sin(2\pi t)Pc_m
\]
gives
\begin{align}
v^\star(x,t)
&=
u(x,t)
+
\left(
\epsilon_0+\frac{\lambda}{2}
\right)
\sin(2\pi t)Pc_m.
\end{align}

Strict convexity of the quadratic term implies uniqueness.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Proposition~\ref{prop:optimal_predictor} gives the population minimizer
with effective watermark strength $\epsilon_0 + \lambda/2$. The empirical
detection scores (Table~\ref{tab:separation}) exceed this predicted value,
which is expected: the parametric model $v_\theta$ trained with finite data and stochastic optimization does not exactly recover the population
optimum, and may allocate additional capacity to the watermark signal
beyond the level predicted by the unconstrained population objective.
Understanding this gap quantitatively is left to future work.
\end{remark}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Model Watermarking via Time-Modulated Random Coding}
\label{alg:watermark}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Input:} Training pairs $(x_0, x_1)$, key $K = (P, C)$, message $m$, 
\State \hspace{\algorithmicindent} watermark strength $\epsilon_0$, loss weight $\lambda$
\State Sample interpolation $x_t = (1-t)x_0 + t x_1$ and ground-truth velocity $u = x_1 - x_0$
\State Retrieve codeword $c_m$ from codebook $C$
\State Define time-modulated watermark: $s(x,t) \leftarrow \sin(2\pi t) \cdot P c_m$
\State Define watermarked training target: $\tilde{v}(x_t,t) \leftarrow u + \epsilon_0 \, s(x_t,t)$
\State Initialize model $v_\theta$
\For{each training step}
    \State Sample batch $\{(x_t^{(i)}, t^{(i)}, \tilde{v}^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^B$
    \State Forward pass: $\hat{v}^{(i)} \leftarrow v_\theta(x_t^{(i)}, t^{(i)})$
    \State Velocity loss: $\mathcal{L}_{\text{vel}} \leftarrow \frac{1}{B}\sum_i \|\hat{v}^{(i)} - \tilde{v}^{(i)}\|^2$
    \State Project: $z^{(i)} \leftarrow P^T \hat{v}^{(i)}$
    \State Demodulate: $d^{(i)} \leftarrow \sin(2\pi t^{(i)}) \cdot z^{(i)}$
    \State Watermark loss: $\mathcal{L}_{\text{wm}} \leftarrow -\frac{1}{B}\sum_i \langle d^{(i)}, c_m \rangle$
    \State Total loss: $\mathcal{L} \leftarrow \mathcal{L}_{\text{vel}} + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{\text{wm}}$
    \State Update $\theta$ via gradient descent
\EndFor
\State \textbf{Output:} Watermarked model $v_{\theta,K,m}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Detection via Synchronous Demodulation}\label{sec:detection}

At detection time, we are given a model $v$ (which may be watermarked
or clean) and the secret key $K = (P, C)$. Detection requires only
black-box query access to $v$.

We estimate the watermark signature via synchronous demodulation. Given
$N$ random query points $(x_i, t_i)$, the signature is computed as
\begin{equation}
\hat{s} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \sin(2\pi t_i) \cdot P^T v(x_i, t_i).
\end{equation}
This estimator combines projection into the latent code space with
synchronous demodulation of the temporal carrier. The multiplication by
$\sin(2\pi t_i)$ extracts the time-coherent component while averaging out
background variation uncorrelated with the carrier, since
$\mathbb{E}[\sin(2\pi t)] = 0$.

The embedded message is recovered by maximum-correlation decoding:
\begin{equation}
\hat{m} = \arg\max_{m \in \mathcal{M}} \langle \hat{s}, c_m \rangle.
\end{equation}
This compares the extracted signature with every codeword. For
$L>\log_2 k$, exact orthogonality is impossible; with an overcomplete
codebook, the margin to the nearest competing message can shrink as $L$
increases.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Watermark Detection via Synchronous Demodulation}
\label{alg:detection}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Input:} Model $v$ to test, key $K = (P, C)$, number of queries $N$
\State Sample $N$ random points: $(x_i, t_i) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 4I_D) \times \text{Uniform}(0,1)$
\State Query model: $y_i \leftarrow v(x_i, t_i)$ for $i = 1, \dots, N$
\State Project to code space: $z_i \leftarrow P^T y_i \in \mathbb{R}^k$
\State Demodulate with carrier: $d_i \leftarrow \sin(2\pi t_i) \cdot z_i$
\State Compute signature: $\hat{s} \leftarrow \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N d_i \in \mathbb{R}^k$
\State Decode message: $\hat{m} \leftarrow \arg\max_{m \in \mathcal{M}} \langle \hat{s}, c_m \rangle$
\State \textbf{Output:} Decoded message $\hat{m}$ and confidence score $\max_m \langle \hat{s}, c_m \rangle$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The effectiveness of the detector follows from the structure of the
watermark. Under a watermarked model, the demodulated signature
concentrates around a scaled version of the embedded codeword; under a
clean model, it remains near zero. A formal analysis of the expected
response and convergence rate is provided in the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:capacity} (Appendix~\ref{app:capacity_proof}). In
summary, $\mathbb{E}[\hat{s}] = \frac{\epsilon}{2} c_m$ for watermarked
models and $\mathbb{E}[\hat{s}] = 0$ for clean models, with estimation
error $O(1/\sqrt{N})$.

\subsection{Information Carried by the Demodulated Channel}

The projected velocity signature defines a noisy channel whose input is
one of the chosen message codewords. A channel-capacity expression alone
does not specify the performance of a particular finite codebook.

\begin{theorem}[Finite-codebook information bound]
\label{thm:capacity}
Let $M$ be drawn from a finite message set and let $c_M\in\mathbb R^k$
be its unit-norm codeword. Assume the demodulated signature is described
by the additive Gaussian surrogate
\begin{equation}
\hat{s} = \frac{\epsilon}{2}c_M+\xi,\qquad
\xi\sim\mathcal N(0,\Sigma/N),
\end{equation}
with $\xi$ independent of $M$ and $\Sigma$ positive definite. Writing
$Q=\operatorname{Cov}(c_M)$, the information conveyed by this codebook
obeys, in nats,
\begin{equation}
I(M;\hat{s})\leq
\min\!\left\{H(M),\;
\frac12\log\det\!\left(I_k+
\frac{N\epsilon^2}{4}\Sigma^{-1/2}Q\Sigma^{-1/2}\right)\right\}.
\end{equation}
The Gaussian-input capacity under the same unit total-power constraint
is obtained by maximizing the log-determinant over positive semidefinite
input covariances $Q$ with $\operatorname{tr}Q\leq1$; it is an upper
bound, not the achieved rate of the finite codebook.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
See Appendix~\ref{app:capacity_proof}.
\end{proof}

The projection dimension $k$ specifies the available signaling space.
The codebook determines how messages occupy that space, represented in
the theorem by $Q=\operatorname{Cov}(c_M)$; the projected velocity noise
enters through $\Sigma$. Their joint spectrum sets the Gaussian bound
at a given query budget. For an explicit pairwise-orthogonal codebook,
at most $k$ messages can occupy $\mathbb R^k$, corresponding to
$\log_2 k$ bits. This is why the common $k=32$, five-bit experiment
uses 32 orthogonal codewords. The longer-payload explicit-codebook
runs in Table~\ref{tab:app-payload} place more codewords in the same
space and therefore relax pairwise orthogonality.

The hypercube instead assigns one orthonormal axis to each bit:
$c_m=L^{-1/2}\sum_{j=1}^L(2m_j-1)e_j$, with $L\leq k$.
It places $2^L$ distinct, generally nonorthogonal codewords on these
$L$ axes. Under uniformly distributed messages,
$Q=L^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^Le_je_j^T$. Thus increasing $L$ uses more of the
available dimensions while dividing unit codeword energy among more
bit coordinates. For isotropic noise $\Sigma=\sigma^2I_k$, the
theorem gives the Gaussian bound
$\frac L2\log(1+N\epsilon^2/(4L\sigma^2))$, capped by the message
entropy $L\log2$. The bound connects $k$, code geometry, noise, and
query budget; observed recovery additionally depends on the discrete
constellation and decoder. Although $Q$ follows from each codebook,
we do not estimate the projected noise covariance $\Sigma$ for the
trained models; the payload sweeps therefore test this tradeoff
empirically rather than fitting the bound.

\subsection{Security Analysis}\label{sec:security_analysis}

\paragraph{Verification and attack goals.}
The object under verification is a model's velocity field $w(x,t)$, not
an individual generated image. The verifier holds $K=(P,C)$ and has
oracle access to $w(x,t)$. Algorithm~\ref{alg:detection} returns a
decoded message $\hat m_K(w)$ and a score; its argmax always returns
some message, even for a clean model. For a prespecified key $K$ and
target message $m$, define the exact-match ownership decision
$D_{K,m}(w)=\mathbf{1}\{\hat m_K(w)=m\}$. The reported clean false-positive
rate is the fraction of clean-model trials for which $D_{K,m}=1$;
watermarked exact-message recovery is the corresponding acceptance
rate on the marked model. This is an empirical false-positive rate for
that fixed-target rule, not for arbitrary-key or multiple-message
searches. We call an attack successful at \emph{unauthorized decoding}
if an adversary without $K$ recovers $m$; at \emph{removal} if it
produces a modified model $w'$ that preserves useful generation quality
but satisfies $\hat m_K(w')\ne m$; and at \emph{forgery} if it produces
a model without the owner's watermark for which $\hat m_K(w')=m$.
Recovering $K$ (or an equivalent decoding key) is a separate, stronger
key-recovery goal. A service exposing only generated images does not
provide the velocity queries required by this verifier. A deployment
with multiple ownership claims must account for the number of keys
and messages tested; a score threshold may further trade false
positives against missed detections.

These goals mirror the removal and false-attribution questions in
generative watermarking, but the attacked object differs. Tree-Ring
encodes a fingerprint in the sampling noise and detects it from
generated images~\cite{wen2023tree}; Stable Signature embeds a signal
through a fine-tuned decoder and likewise verifies
images~\cite{fernandez2023stable}. An attack using a public autoencoder
has weakened Tree-Ring detection in images~\cite{lin2025crack}, but
image editing or inversion does not directly test our velocity-field
verifier. Model modification is a closer analogue: fine-tuning has
been used to remove Stable Signature while retaining image
quality~\cite{hu2024unstable}. Image-level false attribution has also
been demonstrated against semantic diffusion watermarks~\cite{muller2025forgery};
for our model-level claim, forgery instead concerns a model that passes
the keyed velocity-query test.

For a fixed embedded message $m$, write $u=P c_m\in\mathbb{R}^D$.
Even if the attacker estimates $u$ exactly, the following geometric
ambiguities remain.

\begin{proposition}[Single-message subspace ambiguity]
\label{prop:subspace_search}
Suppose $u\ne0$ and $1<k<D$. Knowledge of $u$ alone does not determine
$\operatorname{span}(P)$. The $k$-dimensional subspaces containing $u$
form a Grassmannian $\operatorname{Gr}(k-1,D-1)$ of dimension
$(k-1)(D-k)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $U=\operatorname{span}(u)$. The quotient space
$\mathbb{R}^D/U$ has dimension $D-1$. For every $k$-dimensional
subspace $S$ containing $U$, the quotient $S/U$ is a $(k-1)$-dimensional
subspace of $\mathbb{R}^D/U$, and conversely the inverse image of
every such subspace is a $k$-dimensional subspace containing $U$.
The correspondence is bijective. The dimension of
$\operatorname{Gr}(k-1,D-1)$~\cite{zheng2002communication} is
$(k-1)((D-1)-(k-1))=(k-1)(D-k)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Orthogonal key ambiguity]\label{prop:P_hidden}
For every orthogonal $Q\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}$, the key pairs
$(P,C)$ and $(P Q,\{Q^T c_j\}_{j\in\mathcal M})$ produce the same
watermark signal for every message $j$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For each $j$, $(P Q)(Q^T c_j)=P(QQ^T)c_j=P c_j$.
The time-dependent carrier is therefore identical under both keys.
\end{proof}

These propositions establish ambiguity of the original key from one
message's signal. They do not bound the cost of removal or forgery. In
particular, an attacker with arbitrary velocity-query access could try
to estimate $u$ directly in output space using the public temporal
carrier $\sin(2\pi t)$, then subtract its estimate from the responses
of a copied model or train a surrogate without the signal. The
unwatermarked velocity also varies with time, so the success of this
estimator is an empirical question. Knowing $u$ need not reveal the
message label assigned by the secret codebook.

\paragraph{Evidence under the stated access model.}
The experiments test complementary properties. The random wrong-key
trial yields $0/50$ correct recoveries across the reported trials
(Table~\ref{tab:detection}), showing key specificity against an
independent projection. Clean-model score distributions are separated
from watermarked scores by at least $8.4\sigma$ in the reported settings
(Table~\ref{tab:separation}). Across the five-message RF-UNet runs,
clean-model target-match rates
were $3.0\pm2.4\%$ on MNIST, $1.0\pm2.0\%$ on CIFAR-10, and
$3.0\pm4.0\%$ on CelebA-64; these estimate false positives for the
fixed-target decision above. In the MNIST MLP
experiment, message
recovery remains $100\%$ after $10{,}000$ training steps
(Appendix~\ref{app:persistence}), establishing persistence during
continued training with the watermark objective active. The measured
FID ratios in Table~\ref{tab:quality} track utility alongside these
detection results. In a separate single-message CIFAR-10 RF-UNet sweep,
recovery is $80\%$ at $16$ velocity queries and $100\%$ from $32$ to
$4{,}096$ queries (20 trials per budget), describing sensitivity to the
verifier's query budget rather than to model modification.

These observations support keyed recovery and persistence under the
tested training procedure. Clean continuation after embedding is a
different, directly relevant model-modification test: the released
\texttt{flow-unet-finetune} protocol resumes training without the
watermark loss and measures message recovery and FID after
$0$, $100$, $500$, and $1{,}000$ steps. Its outcomes should be reported
once those runs are complete. For LoRA deployments, verification applies
to the composed base model and adapter; distributing the adapter
separately also permits a recipient to revert to the unwatermarked base.
Neither the wrong-key trial nor watermark-aware continuation measures
adaptive signal estimation or forged ownership claims.

\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}

We evaluate five-bit message recovery across model families, then study
payload scaling under fixed codebook settings. Exact-message recovery,
the clean false-positive rate for a prespecified key and target message,
and FID ratio are the primary outcomes. Wrong-key recovery and score
separation are security diagnostics; nearest-competitor margins explain
payload failures but are not substitutes for exact recovery.

\section{Conclusion}

We have presented a method for embedding verifiable, multi-bit messages
directly into the learned dynamics of flow matching generative models.
The watermark is a key-dependent perturbation of the velocity field,
recovered through synchronous demodulation from black-box velocity
queries, and
uses a zero-mean temporal carrier to avoid a persistent velocity bias.

Five-bit experiments across the tested MLP, RF-UNet, and DiT settings
show $100\%$ message recovery with FID ratios near each model's clean
reference. At fixed $k=128$, the longer-payload sweep reveals a
substantial drop in exact recovery between 64 and 128 bits despite
similar FID ratios. The limited random wrong-key trials yield no
correct recoveries. Extending training to
$10{,}000$ steps does not degrade watermark recovery; a persistence
analysis is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:persistence}.

Several directions remain open. A rigorous finite-sample analysis of the
demodulated channel would sharpen the capacity guarantees. The released
clean-continuation protocol provides a direct next measurement of
watermark persistence after unmarked fine-tuning. Extending the
approach to other generative model families is another next step. The dynamics-level perspective
introduced here, treating the learned velocity field as a continuous
communication channel, may also prove useful beyond watermarking.

\section{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:capacity}}
\label{app:capacity_proof}

We first state the approximation leading to the additive channel, then
prove the information bound for an arbitrary finite codebook. The bound
is conditional on the Gaussian surrogate, not a claim that trained
watermarks realize an exactly additive channel.

\subsection{Step 1: Approximate Effective Channel}

Given a watermarked model $v_{\theta,K,m}$, we query it at $N$ random
points $\{(x_i, t_i)\}_{i=1}^N$ drawn i.i.d.~from a sampling distribution
$p(x,t)$ and compute the signature:
\begin{equation}
\hat{s} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \sin(2\pi t_i) \cdot P^T v_{\theta,K,m}(x_i, t_i).
\end{equation}

Decompose the velocity field as
$v_{\theta,K,m} = v_\theta + \eta_0 + \epsilon \sin(2\pi t) P c_m$,
where $v_\theta$ is the learned flow, $\eta_0$ represents background
variation from training stochasticity and model approximation error, and
$\epsilon \sin(2\pi t) P c_m$ is the watermark signal. Substituting:

\begin{align}
\hat{s} &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \sin(2\pi t_i) P^T [v_\theta(x_i,t_i) + \eta_0(x_i,t_i)] \\
        &\quad + \frac{\epsilon}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \sin^2(2\pi t_i) P^T P c_m.
\end{align}

Since $P^T P = I_k$ by construction, the second term simplifies to:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\epsilon}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \sin^2(2\pi t_i) \cdot c_m.
\end{equation}

Define the background term for each query:
\begin{equation}
z_i := \sin(2\pi t_i) P^T [v_\theta(x_i, t_i) + \eta_0(x_i, t_i)] \in \mathbb{R}^k.
\end{equation}

We introduce the following assumptions.

\begin{assumption}[Uncorrelated carrier and background]\label{ass:uncorrelated}
The temporal carrier $\sin(2\pi t)$ is uncorrelated with the background
velocity field:
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}_{x,t}[\sin(2\pi t) \cdot P^T(v_\theta(x,t) + \eta_0(x,t))] = 0.
\end{equation}
\end{assumption}

The carrier's zero time average does not by itself imply this
assumption, because the background velocity depends on $t$. The
near-zero clean-model scores in Table~\ref{tab:separation} provide a
limited empirical check for the evaluated query distribution.

\begin{assumption}[Independent and identically distributed queries]\label{ass:iid}
The query points $\{(x_i, t_i)\}_{i=1}^N$ are drawn i.i.d.~from a
distribution $p(x,t)$ under which the background terms $z_i$ have finite
second moments.
\end{assumption}

Assumption~\ref{ass:iid} holds by construction, as queries are sampled
independently. Under this assumption, the $z_i$ are i.i.d.~random vectors
with mean $\mathbb{E}[z_i] = \mathbf{0}$ (by Assumption~\ref{ass:uncorrelated})
and covariance matrix
\begin{equation}
\Sigma := \text{Cov}(z_i) = \mathbb{E}[z_i z_i^T] \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times k}.
\end{equation}

For the watermark term, we have:
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[\sin^2(2\pi t)] = \int_0^1 \sin^2(2\pi t) dt = \frac{1}{2}.
\end{equation}
By the Law of Large Numbers,
$\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \sin^2(2\pi t_i) \to \frac{1}{2}$ as $N \to \infty$.

For the background term, we apply the multivariate Central Limit Theorem
(CLT). Let $\xi := \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N z_i$. Under
Assumptions~\ref{ass:uncorrelated} and~\ref{ass:iid}, as $N \to \infty$:
\begin{equation}
\sqrt{N} \xi \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma),
\end{equation}
where $\xrightarrow{d}$ denotes convergence in distribution. Equivalently,
for large $N$:
\begin{equation}
\xi \approx \mathcal{N}\!\left(0, \frac{\Sigma}{N}\right).
\end{equation}

\begin{assumption}[Gaussian approximation for finite $N$]\label{ass:gaussian}
For the query budgets $N$ used in practice, the distribution of $\xi$ is
well-approximated by $\mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma/N)$.
\end{assumption}

While the CLT guarantees asymptotic normality under the stated moment
condition, its accuracy at finite $N$ depends on the distribution of
$z_i$. We do not formally verify it for the evaluated query budgets.

The empirical average of $\sin^2(2\pi t_i)$ also fluctuates around
$1/2$ at finite $N$. Neglecting this message-dependent fluctuation
and approximating the background average by a Gaussian gives the
surrogate channel used in Theorem~\ref{thm:capacity}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:channel_final}
\boxed{\hat{s} = \frac{\epsilon}{2} c_m + \xi, \quad \xi \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(0, \frac{\Sigma}{N}\right).}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Step 2: The Noise Covariance}

The noise covariance $\Sigma$ captures the background velocity variation
in the projected $k$-dimensional space. Let
$u(x,t) := \sin(2\pi t)[v_\theta(x,t) + \eta_0(x,t)] \in \mathbb{R}^D$.
Then:
\begin{equation}
\Sigma = P^T \, \mathbb{E}_{x,t}[u(x,t) u(x,t)^T] \, P.
\end{equation}

\begin{assumption}[Invertible covariance]\label{ass:invertible}
The projected covariance $\Sigma$ is invertible.
\end{assumption}

Invertibility does not follow solely from $k\ll D$ or from randomizing
$P$: it also requires background variation along every projected
direction. We use it as an assumption in the following bound.

\subsection{Step 3: Finite-Codebook Information Bound}

Let $a=\epsilon/2$, $Z\sim\mathcal N(0,\Sigma/N)$, and
$Y=a c_M+Z$. For the finite message distribution, put
$Q=\operatorname{Cov}(c_M)$. Since $Z$ is independent of $M$,
$\operatorname{Cov}(Y)=a^2Q+\Sigma/N$. Gaussian vectors maximize
differential entropy at fixed covariance~\cite{cover1999elements}; hence
\begin{align}
I(M;Y)
&=h(Y)-h(Z)\\
&\leq\frac12\log\frac{\det(a^2Q+\Sigma/N)}{\det(\Sigma/N)}\\
&=\frac12\log\det\!\left(I_k+
\frac{N\epsilon^2}{4}\Sigma^{-1/2}Q\Sigma^{-1/2}\right).
\end{align}
Also $I(M;Y)\leq H(M)$, proving the first claim. Since every
codeword has unit norm,
$\operatorname{tr}Q=1-\|\mathbb E[c_M]\|^2\leq1$.
Maximizing the Gaussian expression over all positive semidefinite
$Q$ with trace at most one gives the unit-power Gaussian relaxation;
the fixed finite codebook need not attain that maximum.

\subsection{Scaling Analysis}

Let $\nu_1,\ldots,\nu_k$ be the eigenvalues of
$\Sigma^{-1/2}Q\Sigma^{-1/2}$. The Gaussian upper bound is
$\frac12\sum_j\log(1+N\epsilon^2\nu_j/4)$.
For fixed positive definite $\Sigma$ and fixed $Q$, its large-$N$
coefficient is $\operatorname{rank}(Q)/2$, the number of codebook
directions carrying message variation. This is a property of the relaxed
Gaussian expression: the finite-alphabet information $I(M;Y)$ is
always capped by $H(M)$ and approaches that cap as the message
distributions become distinguishable. At finite $N$, the nonzero
$\nu_j$ govern how quickly the bound rises. Correlations in projected
velocity affect these values, but covariance alone does not establish
the reliability of a particular decoder.

For uniform hypercube messages on $L$ orthonormal axes,
$Q=L^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^Le_je_j^T$ and $H(M)=L\log 2$. If additionally
$\Sigma=\sigma^2 I_k$, sign decoding has independent bit-error
probability $\Phi(-\epsilon\sqrt{N}/(2\sigma\sqrt L))$ under the
surrogate, where $\Phi$ is the standard-normal CDF. Exact-message
recovery is then $(1-p_{\rm bit})^L$. This illustrative special case
shows how exact recovery can decrease with $L$ even though the total
codeword energy remains one; it is not fitted to the measured models.

\subsection{Relationship to Experiments}

The theorem bounds information in the assumed Gaussian surrogate; it
does not give an achievable payload for the trained decoder. In our experiments,
the message size is $L = 5$ bits with $k = 32$ and $N = 4096$. The
observed statistical separation ($>8.4\sigma$, Table~\ref{tab:separation})
indicates that the empirical SNR is far above the threshold for reliable
decoding of 5-bit messages. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:app-payload} extends
this check to longer payloads on public rectified-flow UNet checkpoints.
In the additional CIFAR-10 sweep (Table~\ref{tab:app-capacity}), exact
recovery drops sharply from 64 to 128 bits at fixed $k=128$, while the
nearest-competitor margin changes sign. This identifies a failure of the
tested decoder under its stated query protocol, not the
information-theoretic capacity of the velocity channel. Comparisons
across $k=32$ and $k=128$ do not isolate payload length because the
carrier dimension also changes.

\section{Watermark Persistence Under Extended Training}\label{app:persistence}

The 10K-step MNIST MLP results provide evidence that the watermark does
not fade with extended training. Across all six watermarked models trained
for $10{,}000$ steps, detection accuracy remains $100\%$. The velocity
loss decreases from $\sim\!0.41$ (at 5K steps) to $\sim\!0.37$ (at 10K
steps), while the watermark correlation term stabilizes around
$3.55$--$3.67$ throughout the second half of training. This indicates that
the two loss terms $\mathcal{L}_{\text{vel}}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{\text{wm}}$
reach a stable equilibrium: the model continues to improve its fit to the
data distribution without suppressing the watermark signal. We attribute
this to the structure of the watermark perturbation: since
$s_{K,m}(x,t)$ is orthogonal to the clean velocity field in expectation,
the two objectives do not directly compete, and the watermark signal is
maintained as a persistent offset in the learned dynamics.

The watermark correlation term stabilizes despite extended training
(Table~\ref{tab:separation}). In the 10K-step clean-model evaluation,
one of 40 trials decoded to a tested message. This is a target-set match,
not a calibrated watermark-presence false positive.

A natural question is whether message capacity can be increased beyond $k$
bits by using multiple carrier frequencies (frequency-division) or time
segments (time-division). We tested this empirically on MNIST and found
that dividing the channel reduces per-carrier signal strength, degrading
detection accuracy significantly compared to the single-carrier design.

\section{Preservation Analysis}
\label{app:preservation}

This appendix characterizes the effect of the proposed perturbation on
the generated distribution. The main result is a first-order bound on
the drift induced by the keyed carrier. Exact endpoint- and
distribution-preserving constructions are discussed afterward as
reference regimes that clarify the tradeoff made by the proposed
watermark. The analysis complements the empirical FID measurements in
Tables~\ref{tab:quality} and~\ref{tab:main} by identifying the leading
factors that control distributional change.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Preliminaries}

Let $u : \R^D \times [0,1] \to \R^D$ be the clean velocity field and
$\varphi_t$ its flow map. Under the noise distribution $p_0$, the flow
induces a density $\rho(\cdot, t) = (\varphi_t)_\# p_0$ satisfying
\begin{equation}
\partial_t \rho + \nabla\!\cdot\!(\rho\,u) = 0,
\label{eq:ce-clean}
\end{equation}
with generative distribution $\rho(\cdot, 1) = (\varphi_1)_\# p_0$.
Write $v = u + \delta u$ for the watermarked velocity, and
$\varphi_t^w$, $\rho^w$ for the corresponding flow map and density.
For a perturbation of strength $\epsilon$, write the corresponding
density as
\begin{equation}
\rho^\epsilon(\cdot,t)
=\rho(\cdot,t)+\epsilon\,\xi(\cdot,t)+O(\epsilon^2),
\label{eq:density-expansion}
\end{equation}
where $\xi$ is the first-order density perturbation. For $0\le s\le
t\le 1$, let $\mathcal{T}_{t\leftarrow s}$ denote the transport
operator associated with the homogeneous part of the linearized
continuity equation around the clean flow. It propagates a density
perturbation from time $s$ to time $t$ under the clean dynamics $u$.
We summarize the worst-case terminal amplification of such perturbations
by
\begin{equation}
M_T = \sup_{0\le r\le 1}\|\mathcal{T}_{1\leftarrow r}\|_{\TV\to\TV},
\label{eq:transport-amplification}
\end{equation}
which is finite under the smooth-flow assumptions used below. This is a
stability quantity for the linearized transport/continuity equation
rather than a standard flow-matching metric; related stability analyses
appear in the transport equation
literature~\cite{diperna1989ordinary,ambrosio2004transport}. In the
flow-matching settings considered here, the source distribution is a
full-dimensional Gaussian and the generated distribution is represented
in the same ambient image or latent space. Thus the clean flow is not
expected to amplify perturbations through a severe dimensional collapse,
and the measured FID ratios are consistent with this stable behavior.

The proposed perturbation is
\begin{equation}
\delta u(x,t) = \epsilon\,\alpha(t)\,P c_m,
\qquad \alpha(t) = \sin(2\pi t),
\label{eq:proposed}
\end{equation}
with $P \in \R^{D \times k}$ semi-orthogonal, $c_m$ a unit codeword,
and $\epsilon > 0$.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{First-Order Distribution Drift}

The proposed carrier adds a fixed keyed direction to the velocity field.
It is therefore natural to analyze the resulting change in the terminal
density directly. The following bound is the preservation statement used
for the proposed method.

\begin{theorem}[First-order drift bound]
\label{thm:drift}
Assume $u$, $\rho$, and $\delta u$ are sufficiently smooth, and write
$q=P c_m$. Then, for $\delta u(x,t)=\epsilon\alpha(t)q$,
\begin{equation}
\bigl\|\rho^w(\cdot,1) - \rho(\cdot,1)\bigr\|_{\TV}
\;\le\;
\epsilon\,M_T
\int_0^1 |\alpha(r)|\,\|q^\top\nabla\rho(\cdot,r)\|_{\TV}\,dr
\,+\,O(\epsilon^2).
\label{eq:drift-bound}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $\rho^\epsilon=\rho^w$ be the density induced by
$u+\epsilon\alpha(t)q$. In the expansion
\eqref{eq:density-expansion}, $\xi(\cdot,0)=0$ because the clean and
perturbed flows use the same initial distribution. Substituting
\eqref{eq:density-expansion} into the
continuity equation
\begin{equation}
\partial_t\rho^\epsilon
+\nabla\!\cdot\!\left(\rho^\epsilon(u+\epsilon\alpha(t)q)\right)=0
\end{equation}
and collecting first-order terms in $\epsilon$ gives
\begin{equation}
\partial_t\xi+\nabla\!\cdot(\xi u)
=
-\alpha(t)\nabla\!\cdot(\rho q).
\label{eq:linearized-continuity}
\end{equation}
Since $q$ is independent of $x$,
$\nabla\!\cdot(\rho q)=q^\top\nabla\rho$. Duhamel's principle for the
linear equation \eqref{eq:linearized-continuity} yields
\begin{equation}
\xi(\cdot,1)
=
-\int_0^1 \alpha(r)\,
\mathcal{T}_{1\leftarrow r}\!\left[
q^\top\nabla\rho(\cdot,r)
\right]\,dr .
\end{equation}
Therefore
\begin{equation}
\|\rho^\epsilon(\cdot,1)-\rho(\cdot,1)\|_{\TV}
\le
\epsilon\|\xi(\cdot,1)\|_{\TV}+O(\epsilon^2),
\end{equation}
and the triangle inequality gives
\begin{equation}
\|\xi(\cdot,1)\|_{\TV}
\le
\int_0^1 |\alpha(r)|\,
\|\mathcal{T}_{1\leftarrow r}[q^\top\nabla\rho(\cdot,r)]\|_{\TV}\,dr .
\end{equation}
Using the definition of $M_T$ yields \eqref{eq:drift-bound}. The sign is
irrelevant because the total-variation norm is used.
\end{proof}

The bound identifies the leading factors that can change the terminal
distribution: the temporal carrier, the transport of perturbations
through the clean flow, and the alignment between the watermark
direction and the density gradient. This decomposition motivates the
use of a small keyed carrier in a high-dimensional subspace: preservation
is favored when the clean flow has moderate transport amplification and
the keyed direction has limited alignment with the evolving density
gradient, while detectability is retained through matched keyed queries.

\begin{corollary}[Zero mean removes the time-invariant drift component]
\label{cor:zero-mean-gap}
Let
\[
G_q(r)=
\mathcal{T}_{1\leftarrow r}\!\left[
q^\top\nabla\rho(\cdot,r)
\right].
\]
If $\int_0^1\alpha(r)\,dr=0$, then for any signed measure $\bar G$,
\begin{equation}
\xi(\cdot,1)
=
-\int_0^1 \alpha(r)\,\bigl(G_q(r)-\bar G\bigr)\,dr .
\label{eq:zero-mean-residual}
\end{equation}
Consequently, the zero-mean carrier cancels the time-invariant component
of the first-order forcing. If, in addition, $G_q$ is $L_q$-Lipschitz in
total variation, then choosing $\bar G=G_q(1/2)$ and
$\alpha(r)=\sin(2\pi r)$ gives
\begin{equation}
\|\xi(\cdot,1)\|_{\TV}
\le
\frac{L_q}{2\pi}.
\label{eq:zero-mean-lipschitz}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
From the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:drift}, the first-order density
perturbation at time $1$ is
\begin{equation}
\xi(\cdot,1)
=
-\int_0^1 \alpha(r)\,
G_q(r)\,dr .
\end{equation}
Because $\int_0^1\alpha(r)\,dr=0$,
$\int_0^1\alpha(r)\bar G\,dr=0$ for any $\bar G$, which proves
\eqref{eq:zero-mean-residual}. If
$\|G_q(r)-G_q(s)\|_{\TV}\le L_q|r-s|$, then
\[
\|\xi(\cdot,1)\|_{\TV}
\le
L_q\int_0^1 |\sin(2\pi r)|\,|r-1/2|\,dr
= \frac{L_q}{2\pi},
\]
which proves \eqref{eq:zero-mean-lipschitz}.
\end{proof}

The corollary gives the mathematical role of the zero-integral carrier
in the preservation analysis. It removes the component of the transported
forcing that is constant over time; the remaining first-order drift is
controlled by how much the transported density-gradient term varies along
the trajectory. Thus zero mean is useful but not sufficient for exact
preservation, and empirical FID remains the relevant quality measure.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Interpretation of the Drift Bound}
\label{sec:decomp}

Theorem~\ref{thm:drift} exposes the two quantities that determine the
first-order distribution drift. The first is $M_T$, the maximum
amplification of density perturbations by the clean flow. The second is
the alignment integral
\[
\int_0^1 |\alpha(r)|\,\|P c_m\cdot\nabla\rho(\cdot,r)\|_{\TV}\,dr,
\]
which measures how much the watermark direction overlaps with the
time-dependent density gradient. These two terms have different roles:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Amplification.} $M_T$ measures
the maximum expansion of perturbations by the clean flow. For a
well-conditioned flow, this factor is $O(1)$. This is the regime we
expect for the tested flow-matching models because they transport a
full-dimensional Gaussian source to samples in the same ambient space,
rather than collapsing mass onto a low-dimensional endpoint. Empirically,
successful watermark detection does not coincide with a systematic
increase in FID.
\item \textbf{Alignment.} The integral measures the overlap between the
watermark direction and the density gradient across time. If $P c_m$ is
drawn independently of any fixed gradient direction, the typical
projection is smaller in high dimension, scaling like an inverse
square-root dimension factor.
\item \textbf{Carrier.} $\|\alpha\|_\infty = 1$ is fixed by the choice
of $\alpha(t) = \sin(2\pi t)$.
\end{enumerate}

The decomposition suggests why the proposed perturbation can remain
small in distributional effect: in high-dimensional latent spaces, a
random $P c_m$ is expected to have limited alignment with any fixed
density-gradient direction. This intuition is consistent with the FID
ratios in Tables~\ref{tab:quality} and~\ref{tab:main}, but the FID
measurements are the empirical evidence for quality preservation.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Relation to Exact Regimes}

The drift bound above does not claim exact preservation. For comparison,
we distinguish two exact notions. A perturbation $\delta u$ preserves
endpoints if $\varphi_1^w(x_0)=\varphi_1(x_0)$ for every
$x_0\sim p_0$, and it preserves the distribution if
$\rho^w(\cdot,1)=\rho(\cdot,1)$. These notions correspond to two
standard sufficient conditions.

\begin{proposition}[Time reparameterizations preserve endpoints]
\label{prop:parallel}
Let $\tau : [0,1]\to[0,1]$ be differentiable with $\tau(0)=0$,
$\tau(1)=1$, and $\dot{\tau}(t)>0$. If
$v(x,t)=\dot{\tau}(t)u(x,\tau(t))$, then $\varphi_1^w=\varphi_1$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For any $x_0$, set $\psi_t(x_0)=\varphi_{\tau(t)}(x_0)$. Then
$\psi_0(x_0)=x_0$ and
\begin{equation}
\frac{d}{dt}\psi_t(x_0)
=\dot{\tau}(t)u(\varphi_{\tau(t)}(x_0),\tau(t))
=v(\psi_t(x_0),t).
\end{equation}
Thus $\psi_t$ and $\varphi_t^w$ solve the same initial-value problem.
By uniqueness, $\varphi_1^w=\psi_1=\varphi_{\tau(1)}=\varphi_1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Divergence-free perturbations preserve the distribution]
\label{prop:divfree}
Let $\rho$ be the clean density. If
$\nabla\!\cdot(\rho\,\delta u)=0$ for all $(x,t)$, then
$\rho^w(\cdot,1)=\rho(\cdot,1)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since $\rho$ satisfies
$\partial_t\rho+\nabla\!\cdot(\rho u)=0$, the condition
$\nabla\!\cdot(\rho\,\delta u)=0$ implies
\begin{equation}
\partial_t\rho+\nabla\!\cdot\bigl(\rho(u+\delta u)\bigr)=0.
\end{equation}
Thus $\rho$ also solves the continuity equation induced by
$v=u+\delta u$, with the same initial density $p_0$. Uniqueness of the
continuity equation under the assumed regularity gives
$\rho^w(\cdot,t)=\rho(\cdot,t)$ for all $t$.
\end{proof}

These two regimes are useful theoretically, but they are not used as
empirical baselines in this paper: the parallel carrier changes the
temporal representation problem as the payload grows, while the
divergence-free carrier requires an auxiliary density model.
Table~\ref{tab:regimes} summarizes the resulting tradeoff.

\label{tab:regimes}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}

Regime & Endpoint exact & Distribution exact & Multi-bit & Auxiliary model \\
\midrule

\paragraph{Parallel construction.}
We replace \eqref{eq:proposed} with $\delta u(x,t) = a_m(t)\,u(x,t)$,
where $a_m(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{L} b_{m,i}\,\sin(2\pi(i+1)t)$ and
$b_{m,i} \in \{\pm 1\}$ is the $i$-th bit of message $m$. This
preserves the endpoint exactly (Proposition~\ref{prop:parallel}) but
requires the model to represent the high-frequency carrier
$\sin(2\pi(L+1)t)$. This makes it a poor match for a concise
multi-bit ownership watermark: increasing the payload also increases
the highest temporal frequency that must be learned.

\paragraph{Divergence-free construction.}
We replace \eqref{eq:proposed} with $\delta u(x,t) = \epsilon\,\alpha(t)\,w(x,t)$
where $w(x,t)$ is a vector field satisfying $\nabla\!\cdot\!(\rho\,w) = 0$
at every $(x,t)$. This preserves the distribution exactly
(Proposition~\ref{prop:divfree}) but requires access to, or an
estimate of, the intermediate density $\rho(\cdot,t)$. In a practical
implementation, such an estimate would usually require an auxiliary
density model, making the watermark depend on density-estimation
accuracy as well as the base generator.

The comparison identifies the tradeoff: the parallel regime gives exact
endpoint preservation but makes larger payloads harder to represent;
the divergence-free regime gives exact distribution preservation but
requires density information; the proposed construction gives up exact
preservation in exchange for a simple keyed multi-bit carrier whose
first-order drift is controlled by Theorem~\ref{thm:drift}.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Summary}

The proposed construction preserves neither the endpoint nor the
distribution exactly. It satisfies a first-order bound controlled by the
transport amplification $M_T$ and by the alignment between the watermark
direction $P c_m$ and the time-dependent density gradient
$\nabla\rho(\cdot,t)$. Among the three regimes of
Table~\ref{tab:regimes}, it is the only one that carries multi-bit
messages with no auxiliary model. Empirical drift-factor estimation is
left as a separate ablation; here, sample-quality preservation is
supported by the measured FID ratios rather than by this first-order
analysis alone.

\paragraph{Fixed-carrier payload sweeps.}
The first block of Table~\ref{tab:app-capacity} holds $k=32$ and the
explicit-codeword decoder fixed while varying $L$ from 5 to 20.
The nearest-competitor margin decreases from $0.478$ to $0.197$, while
exact-message recovery remains at 100\% in the evaluated trials. This
shows reduced separation, not an observed recovery boundary. The
5-bit row uses QR-orthogonalized random codewords; the longer rows
use independently sampled normalized vectors. Explicitly storing and
searching $2^L$ codewords becomes impractical at larger $L$, motivating
the implicit hypercube realization of the keyed random code. With
orthonormal code-space axes
$e_1,\ldots,e_L\in\mathbb{R}^k$ and $L\leq k$, it assigns message
$m\in\{0,1\}^L$ the unit codeword
$c_m=L^{-1/2}\sum_{j=1}^{L}(2m_j-1)e_j$.
The detector reads each bit from the sign of the corresponding
demodulated coordinate, without enumerating $2^L$ messages or applying
error correction. The $k=32$, 32-bit row is a single feasibility point
for this construction. The last block holds the hypercube code and
$k=128$ fixed while varying $L$ from 64 to 128; only this last pair
isolates payload length at the higher dimension. The constructions have
different codeword geometries and margin scales, so their margins should
not be compared numerically across blocks.

\paragraph{Measured velocity scale.}
For the $k=128$ runs, we compare the trained watermarked model $v_w$
with its shared base model $v_0$ on the same 4,096 held-out CIFAR-10
noise--image interpolation points. Let $d=P c_m$ and
$\alpha_i=\sin(2\pi t_i)$. We report the RMS model-change ratio
$r_{\rm change}$ and its carrier-aligned component $r_{\rm carr}$:
\begin{equation}
r_{\rm change}=
\sqrt{\frac{\sum_i\|v_w(x_i,t_i)-v_0(x_i,t_i)\|_2^2}
{\sum_i\|v_0(x_i,t_i)\|_2^2}},\qquad
r_{\rm carr}=
\frac{\left|\sum_i\alpha_i\langle v_w(x_i,t_i)-v_0(x_i,t_i),d\rangle\right|}
{\sqrt{\sum_i\alpha_i^2}\sqrt{\sum_i\|v_0(x_i,t_i)\|_2^2}}.
\end{equation}
Across five messages, $r_{\rm carr}$ is $1.873\pm0.023\%$ at 64 bits
and $1.897\pm0.018\%$ at 128 bits, while $r_{\rm change}$ is
$12.20\pm0.21\%$ and $11.99\pm0.29\%$, respectively. The latter
includes all changes caused by fine-tuning and should not be identified
with the watermark carrier alone. Relative to the base velocity's
RMS along the keyed direction $d$, the carrier component is
$1.066\pm0.019$ and $1.044\pm0.044$ times as large. Thus the carrier
is small relative to the full velocity field but not relative to the
single coordinate used for detection; similar aggregate strength at
the two payload sizes does not imply equally reliable recovery of
every bit.
